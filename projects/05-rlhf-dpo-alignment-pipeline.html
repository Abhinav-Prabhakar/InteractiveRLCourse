RLHF / DPO Alignment Pipeline

Build a miniature alignment stack — pairwise preferences, a Bradley–Terry reward model, PPO with a KL leash, and DPO — and watch the same judgments move two policies.

01The pipeline, end to end

A pretrained language model predicts the next token. Nothing in that objective says which of two answers a person would rather read. Alignment inserts a preference signal between the model and the update rule.

π_ref→ pairs→ r_φ→ PPO / DPO→ π_θ

Preferences are cheap: a person sees two completions and picks one. The Bradley–Terry model turns those choices into a scalar reward, and the reward turns into a policy. The policy is leashed to the reference by a KL penalty, because the reward model is only trustworthy near the data it saw.

$$\max_{\pi_\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r_\phi(x,y)\big]\;-\;\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\big\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\Big]$$
Deliverable. A blog-style write-up with before/after samples, a reward-margin-versus-KL comparison, and a slider that changes what the annotators prefer.
  1. Generate preferences. Put 36 candidate responses on a two-dimensional landscape and sample pairwise judgments from a hidden reward.
  2. Fit the reward model. Train $r_\phi$ on the pairs with the Bradley–Terry logistic loss and draw the learned landscape.
  3. Run PPO-RLHF. Sample completions, score them with $r_\phi$, and ascend the KL-regularized objective.
  4. Run DPO. Delete the reward model and optimize the same pairs through the policy's own log-ratios.
  5. Compare. Track reward margin, KL to the reference, held-out accuracy, and the ranked samples each policy produces.

Everything below runs in the page at toy scale: 36 responses, a twelve-parameter reward model, and policies that are softmax distributions over those responses. The losses are the ones in Chapter 22.

02Preferences are the dataset

Fix one prompt. Each candidate response $y_i$ sits at a latent point $(u_i,v_i)$: $u$ is helpfulness, $v$ is verbosity. An annotator sees a pair and picks the answer they prefer. Bradley–Terry says the pick is a coin weighted by the reward gap:

$$P\big(y_w\succ y_l\mid x\big)=\sigma\big(r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l)\big),\qquad \sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$$

The slider below changes the hidden reward the annotators use. Slide it left and they reward correctness; slide it right and they reward length. The pair labels are regenerated, so the dataset itself changes.

Read the equation again. Only the difference $r_w-r_l$ appears. Add a constant to every reward and no probability changes: the reward is identified only up to a shift. The reward model will inherit that freedom, and DPO will cancel it exactly.

03Fit a reward model

Give the reward model a small feature map — a bias, two linear terms, and nine radial bumps — and fit its weights by maximum likelihood on the pairs:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RM}}(\phi)=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)\Big]$$

The gradient is the Bradley–Terry residual times the feature difference. When the model already ranks a pair correctly with confidence, $\sigma(-m)$ is near zero and the pair teaches almost nothing.

reward_model.pypython
import numpy as np

# 36 candidate responses on a 2-D latent space: (helpfulness, verbosity)
U, V = np.meshgrid(np.linspace(-.85, .85, 6), np.linspace(-.85, .85, 6))
O = np.stack([U.ravel(), V.ravel()], axis=1)

CENTERS = [(cu, cv) for cu in (-.6, 0, .6) for cv in (-.6, 0, .6)]

def phi(u, v):
    """12 features: bias, two linear terms, nine RBF bumps."""
    rbf = [np.exp(-((u - cu)**2 + (v - cv)**2) / (2 * .5**2))
           for cu, cv in CENTERS]
    return np.array([1., u, v] + rbf)

FE = np.stack([phi(u, v) for u, v in O])            # (36, 12)
pairs = sample_preferences(lam=.3, n=260)           # (n, 2), columns w, l

def bradley_terry_loss(w, pairs):
    r = FE @ w
    margin = r[pairs[:, 0]] - r[pairs[:, 1]]        # winner - loser
    return -np.mean(np.log(1 / (1 + np.exp(-margin))))

def rm_grad(w, pairs):
    r = FE @ w
    margin = r[pairs[:, 0]] - r[pairs[:, 1]]
    s = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-margin))                   # P(winner beats loser)
    coef = -(1 - s)                                 # d loss / d margin
    g = np.zeros_like(w)
    np.add.at(g, pairs[:, 0], coef[:, None] * FE[pairs[:, 0]])
    np.add.at(g, pairs[:, 1], -coef[:, None] * FE[pairs[:, 1]])
    return g / len(pairs)

w = np.zeros(12)
for step in range(450):
    g = rm_grad(w, pairs) + 3e-4 * w                # tiny ridge pins the constant
    w -= .5 * g
    if np.linalg.norm(g) < 1e-3:
        break

The reward model is a proxy. It is smooth wherever it has evidence and extrapolates everywhere else. Optimize it hard enough and the policy finds the extrapolation — fluent, confident, wrong answers that score beautifully. The KL leash exists to keep the policy inside the calibrated region.

04PPO-RLHF and DPO, side by side

PPO keeps the reward model and treats alignment as an RL problem. Each step samples completions, scores them with $r_\phi$, and ascends the KL-regularized objective. The KL term enters as a penalty on the policy's drift:

$$\nabla_\theta J=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y)\,r_\phi(y)\big]-\beta\,\nabla_\theta D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_\theta\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\big)$$

DPO never builds $r_\phi$. Substitute the implicit reward $\hat r_\theta=\beta\log(\pi_\theta/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$ into the Bradley–Terry likelihood and the reward model disappears:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\Big]$$

Its gradient is one supervised step with a self-weighting term: raise the winner, lower the loser, scaled by how badly the implicit reward still ranks the pair.

ppo_rlhf.pypython
import numpy as np

def ppo_step(z, rm_weights, beta, lr, rng, batch=128):
    p = softmax(z)                                  # policy over 36 responses
    y = rng.choice(len(z), size=batch, p=p)         # sample completions
    r = FE[y] @ rm_weights                          # reward-model scores
    adv = r - r.mean()                              # batch mean baseline

    # score-function gradient of E_pi[r]
    grad = np.zeros_like(z)
    for k in range(len(z)):
        grad[k] = np.mean(((y == k) - p[k]) * adv)

    # exact gradient of the KL leash, then a trust-region clip
    logp = np.log(p + 1e-12)
    KL = np.sum(p * (logp - log_ref))
    grad -= beta * p * (logp - log_ref - KL)
    norm = np.linalg.norm(grad)
    if norm > 1.:
        grad /= norm
    z += lr * grad
    return z, KL
dpo.pypython
def dpo_step(z, pairs, beta, lr):
    logp = log_softmax(z)                           # log pi_theta
    lw = logp[pairs[:, 0]] - log_ref[pairs[:, 0]]
    ll = logp[pairs[:, 1]] - log_ref[pairs[:, 1]]
    margin = beta * (lw - ll)                       # implicit reward gap
    s = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-margin))                   # P(preferred wins)

    g = np.zeros_like(z)
    np.add.at(g, pairs[:, 0], -beta * (1 - s))      # raise the winner
    np.add.at(g, pairs[:, 1],  beta * (1 - s))      # lower the loser
    z -= lr * g / len(pairs)
    return -np.mean(np.log(s + 1e-12))

05Read the reward back out of the policy

The KL-regularized objective has a closed-form optimum, $\pi^\star\propto\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}e^{r/\beta}$. Rearranged, any policy defines a reward:

$$\hat r_\theta(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}=r_\phi(x,y)-\underbrace{\beta\log Z(x)}_{\text{prompt-only constant}}$$

Below, the landscape is that implicit reward. It has the shape of the reward model, shifted by a constant that no preference can see. Move $\beta$: the implicit reward keeps its shape while the policy's mass sharpens or relaxes.

06What to compare, and what to report

One number is never enough. Report the reward margin and the KL that bought it, because a margin without its KL is uninterpretable. Score held-out pairs, and check the proxy against a reward you trust.

Move the preference slider and the whole stack moves with it: the dataset, the reward landscape, both training runs, and the sample rankings. That coupling is the lesson. The algorithm is only as aligned as the comparisons that feed it.
