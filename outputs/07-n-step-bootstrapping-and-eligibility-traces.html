n-Step Bootstrapping
& Eligibility Traces

One reward and a guess, or every reward and no guess — and every mixture in between, computed online from a single error signal.

01The n-step return

TD(0) looks one step ahead and then guesses. Monte Carlo waits for the episode to end and uses everything. The n-step return puts a dial between them: take $n$ real rewards, then bootstrap from an estimate.

$$G_{t:t+n}=R_{t+1}+\gamma R_{t+2}+\cdots+\gamma^{n-1}R_{t+n}+\gamma^{n}V(S_{t+n}) =\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\gamma^{k}R_{t+k+1}+\gamma^{n}V(S_{t+n})$$

The sum is observed; the last term is imagined from $V$. The moment you stop trusting observations and start trusting an estimate, you have bootstrapped. The prediction update is the one you already know, pointed at a longer target:

$$V(S_t)\leftarrow V(S_t)+\alpha\big[G_{t:t+n}-V(S_t)\big]$$

$n=1$ is exactly TD(0). $n\ge T-t$ — the window reaches the end of the episode, where $V(S_T)=0$ — is exactly Monte Carlo. Every integer in between is a legitimate target.

$n$ is a bias–variance dial. Small $n$ keeps variance low but leans hard on a possibly-wrong $V$; large $n$ shrinks the bias but pays for it in the variance of long reward chains. TD($\lambda$), below, averages over every $n$ at once.

02n-step Sarsa

Swap the state value for an action value and the same construction gives n-step Sarsa. The target now names the action actually taken at the horizon — hence the name.

$$G_{t:t+n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\gamma^{k}R_{t+k+1}+\gamma^{n}Q(S_{t+n},A_{t+n}), \qquad Q(S_t,A_t)\leftarrow Q(S_t,A_t)+\alpha\big[G_{t:t+n}-Q(S_t,A_t)\big]$$

$n=1$ recovers Sarsa. For an off-policy target the whole $n$-step return must be reweighted by the importance ratio of the actions in the window, target policy $\pi$ over behavior policy $b$:

$$\rho_{t:t+n-1}=\prod_{k=t}^{\min(t+n-1,\,T-1)}\frac{\pi(A_k\mid S_k)}{b(A_k\mid S_k)}, \qquad V(S_t)\leftarrow V(S_t)+\alpha\,\rho_{t:t+n-1}\big[G_{t:t+n}-V(S_t)\big]$$

For action values the first action $A_t$ is already given, so the ratio starts one step later: $Q(S_t,A_t)\leftarrow Q(S_t,A_t)+\alpha\,\rho_{t+1:t+n}\big[G_{t:t+n}-Q(S_t,A_t)\big]$. For on-policy $n$-step Sarsa the ratios are exactly $1$ — numerator and denominator are the same policy — so no correction is needed.

Off-policy $n$-step learning is where the variance explodes. The weight $\rho_{t:t+n-1}$ is a product of $n$ ratios, and its variance can grow exponentially with $n$. Cutting traces, as Watkins' Q($\lambda$) does below, is one practical escape from that blow-up.

03TD(λ) and the λ-return

Instead of committing to one $n$, average all the $n$-step returns with geometrically decaying weights.

$$G_t^{\lambda}=(1-\lambda)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{n-1}\,G_{t:t+n}$$

The weights $(1-\lambda)\lambda^{n-1}$ are a distribution over horizons: they sum to $1$, put all their mass on $n=1$ when $\lambda=0$, and on the full Monte Carlo return when $\lambda=1$. The $\lambda$-return algorithm is the prediction update built on this target; TD($\lambda$), below, is how it is run online.

$$V(S_t)\leftarrow V(S_t)+\alpha\big[G_t^{\lambda}-V(S_t)\big]$$

This is the forward view: it names the target exactly, but to evaluate $G_t^{\lambda}$ online you would have to look forward over the entire future before making one update. Drag the slider and watch the weights slide between the two extremes.

04Eligibility traces: the backward view

Here is the trick that makes the forward view runnable online. Give every state an eligibility trace $E_t(s)$ — a decaying memory of how recently, and how often, that state was visited.

$$E_t(s)=\gamma\lambda\,E_{t-1}(s)+\mathbf{1}(S_t=s),\qquad E_0(s)=0$$

Read it back: every step each trace fades by $\gamma\lambda$, and the state the agent is standing in receives a bump of $1$. A state visited $k$ steps ago carries $(\gamma\lambda)^{k}$ — a short memory when $\lambda$ is small, a long one when it is large. Then update every state on every step, driven by the one-step TD error:

$$\delta_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma V(S_{t+1})-V(S_t), \qquad V(s)\leftarrow V(s)+\alpha\,\delta_t\,E_t(s)\quad\text{for all }s\in\mathcal{S}$$

This is the backward view: one scalar error, broadcast backward over the trajectory in proportion to eligibility. The load-bearing step is the equivalence. Hold $V$ fixed during the episode and the forward-view error telescopes into discounted TD errors:

$$G_t^{\lambda}-V(S_t)=\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}(\gamma\lambda)^{k-t}\,\delta_k$$

Sum the backward-view increments $\alpha\,\delta_k E_k(s)$ over the episode, expand $E_k(s)=\sum_{t\le k}(\gamma\lambda)^{k-t}\mathbf{1}(S_t=s)$, and swap the order of summation: you get $\alpha\sum_t \mathbf{1}(S_t=s)\,[G_t^{\lambda}-V(S_t)]$ — exactly the forward-view updates. So for tabular accumulating traces the two views agree exactly when updates are applied at episode end (offline). Applied online, step by step, they agree only approximately — closer the smaller $\alpha$ is; true online TD($\lambda$) restores exact equivalence.

The whole story is one line: $\delta_t$ is spread back over the trajectory by $E_t$. The forward view is what it computes; the backward view is how it computes it without ever waiting for the future.

05Sarsa(λ) and Watkins' Q(λ)

Put the traces on state–action pairs and drive them with the Sarsa error, and you have Sarsa($\lambda$) — the on-policy control algorithm.

$$\begin{aligned} \delta_t &= R_{t+1}+\gamma Q(S_{t+1},A_{t+1})-Q(S_t,A_t)\\[2pt] E_t(s,a) &= \gamma\lambda\,E_{t-1}(s,a)+\mathbf{1}(s=S_t,\,a=A_t)\\[2pt] Q(s,a) &\leftarrow Q(s,a)+\alpha\,\delta_t\,E_t(s,a)\quad\text{for all }(s,a) \end{aligned}$$

Watkins' Q($\lambda$) changes the target to the greedy, Q-learning one — $\delta_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma\max_a Q(S_{t+1},a)-Q(S_t,A_t)$ — so the trace rule has to change with it. The $\max$ evaluates an action the behavior policy may never take, and any trace running through such an action would credit a path the target never follows. It must be erased.

$$E_t(s,a)=\begin{cases} \gamma\lambda\,E_{t-1}(s,a)+\mathbf{1}(s=S_t,\,a=A_t), & A_t=\arg\max_{a}Q(S_t,a)\\[2pt] \mathbf{1}(s=S_t,\,a=A_t), & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$
The trace is cut whenever behavior takes a non-greedy action. Every older trace drops to $0$; only the pair just taken keeps its bump. That single guard is what keeps Watkins' Q($\lambda$) off-policy: credit flows back only along action sequences the Q-learning target would actually have taken.

Cheat sheet

$$\begin{aligned} G_{t:t+n} &= \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\gamma^{k}R_{t+k+1}+\gamma^{n}V(S_{t+n})\\[2pt] G_t^{\lambda} &= (1-\lambda)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{n-1}G_{t:t+n}\\[2pt] E_t(s) &= \gamma\lambda\,E_{t-1}(s)+\mathbf{1}(S_t=s)\\[2pt] \delta_t &= R_{t+1}+\gamma V(S_{t+1})-V(S_t)\\[2pt] V(s) &\leftarrow V(s)+\alpha\,\delta_t\,E_t(s) \end{aligned}$$

Everything downstream — GAE in policy gradients, Dyna's planning backups, and the bias–variance dial every deep-RL target still tinkers with — is a choice about how far back to spread $\delta_t$ and how long to remember a state.
