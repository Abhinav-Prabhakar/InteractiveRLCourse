Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback
& LLM Alignment

How a language model learns to prefer what humans prefer — from pairwise judgments to the DPO loss you can derive in five lines.

01The RLHF pipeline

A pretrained language model predicts the next token. It has no idea which completions humans prefer. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) turns pairwise human judgments into a training signal in three stages.

π_base→ SFT→ r_φ→ PPO→ π_θ

Stage 1 — supervised fine-tuning. Clone demonstrations to get a fluent starting policy $\pi_{\mathrm{SFT}}$. It imitates, but imitation has no notion of better.

Stage 2 — reward modeling. Show labelers pairs of answers and ask which they prefer. Fit a scalar network $r_\phi(x,y)$ whose differences reproduce those choices.

Stage 3 — reinforcement learning. Treat $r_\phi$ as the reward, sample completions from the policy, and optimize it — with a leash back to the reference policy:

$$\max_{\pi_\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r_\phi(x,y)\big]\;-\;\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\big\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\Big]$$

$\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is the frozen SFT model; $\beta$ prices every nat of divergence from it.

Why the KL term is not optional. The reward model is fit on a narrow slice of text and is wrong almost everywhere else. Without the leash, the policy finds the errors — degenerate strings that score absurdly high — and fluency collapses. The KL term keeps the policy inside the region where $r_\phi$ was ever calibrated.

02Preferences: the Bradley–Terry model

Labelers rarely give a numeric score; they pick the better of two answers. The Bradley–Terry model turns that comparison into a probability over rewards:

$$P\big(y_w\succ y_l\mid x\big)=\sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\big),\qquad \sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$$

The reward model is trained by maximum likelihood — push the preferred answer's score above the rejected one's:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RM}}(\phi)=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)\Big]$$

Read that back: the log-odds of a preference is exactly the reward gap. Only differences of reward are identified — add any function $c(x)$ to every score and no probability changes.

Drag the margin. Near $\Delta=0$ the sigmoid is steepest: a near-tie is a coin flip, and the loss gradient $\sigma(-\Delta)$ is large. Large positive $\Delta$ saturates the loss and the pair stops teaching; large negative $\Delta$ — a confident wrong ranking — costs linearly and teaches the most.

03The KL-regularized objective has a closed form

The RL stage is a per-prompt optimization. Drop the outer expectation, fix $x$, and write the objective as a single expectation:

$$\max_{\pi}\ \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}\Big[r(x,y)-\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Big]$$

Exponentiate the bracket and divide by a normalizer. The objective rewrites exactly as $-\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi\,\Vert\,\pi^*\big)+\beta\log Z(x)$, whose optimum is the Gibbs distribution:

$$\pi^*(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Big(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\Big),\qquad Z(x)=\sum_{y}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Big(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\Big)$$

This step is exactly where the KL is spent: a KL is non-negative and hits zero only at $\pi=\pi^*$, so the first term vanishes and the second does not depend on $\pi$.

Now solve that identity for the reward. Rearranging — the single load-bearing line of the whole chapter —

$$r(x,y)=\underbrace{\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}}_{\text{implicit reward }\hat r_\theta(x,y)}+\;\beta\log Z(x)$$

Any policy can be read as a reward function, up to the prompt-only constant $\beta\log Z(x)$. That is the identical trick DPO is built on.

The exponent is the whole story. The optimal aligned policy is not a new object — it is the reference policy reweighted by its reward, with $\beta$ setting how hard the reweighting pushes. Hold onto that form: it collapses a reinforcement-learning problem into a supervised one.

04DPO: the reward model you never train

Substitute the implicit reward back into Bradley–Terry. The reward model never appears:

$$P\big(y_w\succ y_l\mid x\big)=\sigma\Big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\Big)=\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)$$

The two $\beta\log Z(x)$ terms are identical and cancel — that cancellation is the whole story. Where the reward model used to sit, we now have the policy's own log-ratios.

So train the policy directly by maximum likelihood on preferences. This is direct preference optimization (DPO):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta)=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\Big]$$

Its gradient is a single supervised step with a learned weight:

$$\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}=-\beta\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\underbrace{\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)}_{\text{how wrong the model still is}}\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\Big]$$

Read the gradient back: it raises the log-probability of the winning answer and lowers the loser's, scaled by how badly the implicit reward currently ranks the pair. Pairs the model already separates barely move it.

No reward model, no sampling, no RL loop. DPO is one classification loss over a fixed preference dataset — the same shape as stage 2 of RLHF, applied to the policy instead of to a reward head. At the population optimum $\hat r_\theta$ recovers the Bradley–Terry reward up to the prompt-only $\beta\log Z(x)$, so both pipelines target the same policy.

05The preference landscape

Fix a prompt and split the output space by two reward-relevant latent features. Let $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ be a broad prior and $r(x,\cdot)$ the reward model's score over the same outputs. The DPO optimum is $\pi_\theta(y)\propto\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\,e^{r/\beta}$ — a tilted, sharpened version of the prior.

06GRPO and verifiable rewards

PPO optimizes the objective of section 01 with an actor–critic setup: a value network $V_\psi$ supplies the baseline — $\hat A_t$ by GAE, roughly $r-V_\psi(s_t)$ — and must be trained alongside the policy, doubling the memory. Group relative policy optimization (GRPO) deletes the critic. For each prompt it samples a group of $G$ completions, scores them, and uses the group's own statistics as the baseline:

$$\hat A_i=\frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(r)}{\operatorname{std}(r)},\qquad i=1,\dots,G$$

The group mean replaces the learned value function; dividing by the group standard deviation makes the scale irrelevant. Everything else is PPO's clipped ratio with a KL leash:

$$\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\min\big(\rho_i\hat A_i,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_i,\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon)\,\hat A_i\big)\Big]-\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(\pi_\theta\,\Vert\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\big),\quad \rho_i=\frac{\pi_\theta(o_i\mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_i\mid x)}$$

Two details of the original. The ratio is per token — each $o_i$'s terms are averaged over its length, all sharing $\hat A_i$ — and the KL is a loss term, not a penalty folded into the reward as in PPO-RLHF.

When rewards are verifiable — a unit test passes, a math answer matches, a proof checks — no reward model is needed at all. RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) feeds a programmatic 0/1 checker straight into GRPO (or PPO), and it is the workhorse behind reasoning models. One catch: a group that is all right or all wrong has $\operatorname{std}(r)=0$ and every $\hat A_i=0$ — no gradient. Prompts must be hard enough to split the group.

A group is a free critic. The baseline you need to reduce variance is just the mean of the group you already sampled. No value network, no bootstrapping, no separate critic loss — the advantage is a statistic of the samples, not a learned prediction.

07Reward hacking, KL drift, evaluation

Reward hacking

Reward hacking is the policy maximizing the proxy $r_\phi$ instead of the goal. The reward model is trained on a finite, biased slice of human preference and is smooth in exactly the wrong places: it rewards length, confident phrasing, and agreement. Optimize it hard enough and you get verbose, sycophantic, confidently-wrong answers that score beautifully. This is Goodhart's law with a gradient.

KL drift

As training proceeds, $\pi_\theta$ drifts away from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. The KL term limits the drift but does not remove it, and past some distance $r_\phi$ is extrapolating. Track the KL every step and stop when it climbs: reward curves that rise while the KL explodes are the signature of a policy exploiting the proxy, not improving. Gao et al. (2023) measured it against a held-out gold reward model: the proxy climbs monotonically with KL, while the gold reward rises, peaks, and falls.

Evaluation

Measure both the proxy and the goal. Report win rate against the SFT reference and a strong baseline, use a length-controlled win rate to strip verbosity bias, and hold out a preference set to score $r_\phi$ itself. Plot reward against KL — the frontier — because a single reward number without its KL is uninterpretable.

The reward model is a proxy, and proxies get gamed. Every alignment method on this page is only as good as the preferences that define it, which is why the field keeps moving from a trained $r_\phi$ toward rewards you can verify: a checker cannot be sweet-talked into high scores.

Cheat sheet

$$\begin{aligned} P(y_w\succ y_l\mid x) &= \sigma\big(r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l)\big)\\[2pt] \pi^*(y\mid x) &= \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\Big(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\Big)\\[2pt] \hat r_\theta(x,y) &= \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}\\[2pt] \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}} &= -\mathbb{E}\Big[\log\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\big)\Big]\\[2pt] \hat A_i &= \frac{r_i-\operatorname{mean}(r)}{\operatorname{std}(r)} \end{aligned}$$

None of it is new machinery. $\beta$ is Chapter 21's price, charged in nats; PPO is Chapter 12's; the group mean is Chapter 11's baseline; fitting $r_\phi$ to choices is Chapter 20's inverse problem. Everything downstream — iterative and online DPO, judge-based RLAIF, process reward models, and the full alignment pipeline in Project 5 — is a way of steering one of these five lines.
