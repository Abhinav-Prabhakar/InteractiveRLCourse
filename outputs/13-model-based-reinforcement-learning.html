Model-Based
Reinforcement Learning

Stop asking the environment what happens. Learn the world once, then answer the question yourself.

01Learning a model

Every method so far asked the environment a question and waited for the answer. A model lets you answer it yourself. Model-based RL learns two functions from transitions: a dynamics model $\hat P_\phi(s'\mid s,a)$ and a reward model $\hat R_\psi(s,a)$.

$$\hat P_\phi,\ \hat R_\psi \;=\; \arg\max_{\phi,\psi}\;\mathbb{E}_{(s,a,r,s')\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\hat P_\phi(s'\mid s,a)\;+\;\log\mathcal{N}\!\big(r;\hat R_\psi(s,a),\sigma^{2}\big)\Big].$$

For deterministic control — a pendulum, a robot arm — the transition is a regression, and the whole model is least squares:

$$\min_\phi\;\mathbb{E}_{(s,a,s')\sim\mathcal{D}}\big\|s' - f_\phi(s,a)\big\|^{2}.$$

One line of supervised learning, then planning forever. The catch is in the distribution of inputs.

A model is supervised learning with adversarial test data. The states you will actually query are the states your own policy chooses, not the states you trained on. The model fits $\mathcal{D}$ perfectly and still fails exactly where planning wants to go.

02Dyna — planning with a model

Dyna keeps the model-free update and adds one more loop. After every real step, sample a state-action pair you have seen, roll it forward through the model for a simulated reward and next state, and apply the same backup to that fabricated transition.

$$\begin{aligned} &\textbf{act:}\quad a=\arg\max_{a'}Q(s,a')\ ;\ \ \text{step the world to }(r,s')\\[2pt] &\textbf{learn:}\quad Q(s,a)\leftarrow Q(s,a)+\alpha\big[r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')-Q(s,a)\big]\\[2pt] &\textbf{plan:}\ \ \text{repeat }n\text{ times:}\quad (s,a)\sim\mathcal{D},\ \ \hat s'\sim\hat P_\phi(\cdot\mid s,a),\ \ \hat r=\hat R_\psi(s,a)\\[2pt] &\phantom{\textbf{plan:}}\quad Q(s,a)\leftarrow Q(s,a)+\alpha\big[\hat r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(\hat s',a')-Q(s,a)\big] \end{aligned}$$

One real transition now produces $n+1$ backups. The third and fourth lines are ordinary TD updates — they simply never touch the environment. The only new ingredient is the sampling of $\hat s'$ from the model instead of the world.

Planning is just learning from data you did not have to collect. That is the entire source of model-based sample efficiency, and the entire source of its risk: the imagined data inherits every error in the model, and it never pushes back.

04Short-horizon control: MPC and shooting

With a model in hand, control becomes optimization. Model-predictive control (MPC) solves a finite-horizon problem, executes one action, and replans from the new state.

$$u^{*}_{0:H-1}=\arg\max_{u_{0:H-1}}\;\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{k}\,\hat r(s_k,u_k)\Big]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad s_{k+1}=f_\phi(s_k,u_k),\qquad u^{*}_0\ \text{executed}.$$

When the action space is continuous and the model is cheap, you do not need gradients. Random shooting samples $K$ action sequences, rolls each one out, and keeps the best. CEM iterates that idea, refitting a Gaussian to the elite samples. PETS wraps the whole loop in a probabilistic ensemble so the optimizer cannot exploit uncertainty.

The optimizer attacks the model. A horizon-$H$ objective has $H$ chances to find a state where the model is optimistic and the world is not. Short horizons and uncertainty penalties are not conservatism for its own sake — they are the only thing standing between a planner and its model's blind spots.

05Latent world models: PETS, MBPO, Dreamer

Pixels are high-dimensional; the dynamics behind them are not. A latent world model compresses each observation into a small state $z_t$ and learns the dynamics there. Planning then happens in the latent space.

PETS keeps the model explicit — an ensemble of probabilistic dynamics models — and plans with MPC, adding a penalty wherever the ensemble disagrees. MBPO trusts the model for only a few steps at a time: it branches short rollouts from real replay states and trains the policy on those, mixing imagined and real data.

$$J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{s_0\in\mathcal{D}}\Big[\sum_{k=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{k}\,\hat r\big(s_k,\pi_\theta(s_k)\big)\Big],\qquad s_{k+1}=f_\phi\big(s_k,\pi_\theta(s_k)\big).$$

Dreamer removes the planner entirely. It learns a recurrent state-space model — encode $z_t$, roll $z_{t+1}\sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid z_t,a_t)$, decode — and trains the policy only on rollouts imagined inside the latent space.

$$\begin{aligned} &\textbf{representation:}\quad z_t\sim q_\theta(z_t\mid o_{\le t},a_{\lt t})\\[2pt] &\textbf{dynamics:}\quad z_{t+1}\sim p_\theta(z_{t+1}\mid z_t,a_t),\qquad \hat r_t\sim q_\theta(r_t\mid z_t)\\[2pt] &\textbf{imagination:}\quad \max_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k\ge 0}\gamma^{k}\,\hat r_\theta(z_k,a_k)\Big] \end{aligned}$$

Because the model is differentiable, the policy gradient flows straight through it. This is the model-based policy gradient: backpropagation through the unrolled dynamics.

$$\nabla_\theta J=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{k}\big(\nabla_{s_{k}}J\big)^{\!\top}\,\nabla_\theta f_\phi\big(s_k,\pi_\theta(s_k)\big)\Big].$$

No environment query appears anywhere in the update — the entire gradient is manufactured inside the model. That is the difference between dreaming and acting.

06MuZero — planning without rules

Dreamer still decodes observations. MuZero refuses even that. It learns a latent model whose only job is to serve the search: it must predict rewards, values, and policies, not pixels.

$$s^{0}=h_\theta(o_{1:t}),\qquad s^{k},\,r^{k}=g_\theta(s^{k-1},a_k),\qquad p^{k},\,v^{k}=f_\theta(s^{k}).$$

Inside the search, MCTS unfolds $g_\theta$ along hypothetical action sequences and uses $p^{k},v^{k}$ to guide the tree. Nothing is ever decoded back to an observation — the state $s^{k}$ is free to mean whatever the search needs it to mean.

The three functions are trained end-to-end by unrolling the model $K$ steps and matching the search's own improved policy $\pi^{k}$, the observed reward $u$, and the game outcome $z$:

$$\mathcal{L}=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\Big[\underbrace{(v^{k}-z)^{2}}_{\text{value}}+\underbrace{\pi^{k\top}\log p^{k}}_{\text{policy}}+\underbrace{(r^{k}-u)^{2}}_{\text{reward}}\Big].$$
MuZero is the punchline of the chapter. The model is no longer a replica of the world — it is a simulator that exists only to be searched, trained by the value it produces. An explicit dynamics model is one way to build it; a neural latent one is another. Both are just $f_\phi$.

07Why the model lies: bias versus variance

Compare the two ways to estimate a return. Model-free Monte Carlo averages actual sampled returns: unbiased, but its variance falls only as $1/n$. Model-based prediction rolls the learned dynamics forward: cheap and low-variance, but every imagined step spends a little of the model's bias.

$$\mathrm{MSE}=\underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat J]-J\big)^{2}}_{\text{bias}^{2}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}[\hat J]}_{\text{variance}},\qquad \big|\hat J-J\big|\;\approx\;\sum_{k=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{k}\,\epsilon_k\ \ \text{for per-step model error }\epsilon_k.$$

The bias compounds through the rollout. If the one-step dynamics error is $\epsilon$ and the model is $L$-Lipschitz, the $k$-step state error is bounded by

$$\|\hat s_k-s_k\|\;\le\;\epsilon\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}L^{i}\;=\;\epsilon\,\frac{L^{k}-1}{L-1}.$$

Read that last line back: the one-step error is multiplied by an exponential in the horizon. When $L>1$ — exactly the interesting regime — a model that is excellent one step out can be useless twenty steps out. That is the blue curve in the rollout above.

Planning buys variance with bias. A short model rollout behaves like a noisy but cheap return estimate; a long one behaves like a confident but wrong one. Every real system — MBPO's short branches, Dreamer's imagination horizon, MuZero's search depth — is one chosen point on that curve.
