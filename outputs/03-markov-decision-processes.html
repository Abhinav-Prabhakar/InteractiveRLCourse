Markov Decision Processes

The tuple every RL algorithm is ultimately trying to solve — a state, a choice, a reward, and a discount, with every transition probability you can poke at.

01The Markov property and the MDP tuple

A stochastic process has the Markov property when the future is conditionally independent of the past given the present:

$$\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{t+1}=s' \;\middle|\; S_t=s,\; S_{t-1}=s_{t-1},\dots,S_0=s_0\right)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{t+1}=s' \;\middle|\; S_t=s\right)$$

Equivalently: the state is a sufficient statistic for the future. Knowing where you are makes how you got there irrelevant.

Why this single assumption carries the whole theory. It lets us write the expected return from a state as a function of that state alone, $v(s)$. Without it, value would have to depend on the entire history, and nothing that follows would close.

An MDP adds a decision. It is the tuple $(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,R,\gamma)$ — states, actions, and the new ingredient: a joint transition-and-reward kernel indexed by the action taken,

$$p(s',r\mid s,a)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{t+1}=s',\,R_{t+1}=r \mid S_t=s,\,A_t=a\right),\qquad \sum_{s'\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}p(s',r\mid s,a)=1 .$$

The discount $\gamma\in[0,1]$ trades immediate reward against future reward. Everything the agent can do is contained in $(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},p,\gamma)$; everything it wants to know is a number attached to a state or a state–action pair.

02Markov chains and the transition matrix

Strip away the actions and the rewards and the skeleton that remains is a Markov chain: a set of states $\mathcal{S}$ plus a transition matrix whose entries are

$$P_{ss'}=\mathbb{P}\!\left(S_{t+1}=s' \mid S_t=s\right),\qquad P_{ss'}\ge 0,\qquad \sum_{s'\in\mathcal{S}}P_{ss'}=1 .$$

Every row of $P$ is a probability distribution. The $n$-step transition matrix is just $P^n$, and a distribution $\pi$ is stationary if $\pi^\top P=\pi^\top$. The MDP's action kernel $p(\cdot\mid\cdot,a)$ is a stack of exactly these matrices — one per action.

03Returns and the discount

The return $G_t$ is the total discounted reward from time $t$ onward:

$$G_t \;=\; R_{t+1}+\gamma R_{t+2}+\gamma^2R_{t+3}+\cdots \;=\;\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}R_{t+k+1}.$$

The discount does two jobs: it keeps the sum finite, and it encodes how much we care about the future. Drag the slider and watch how one reward sequence changes value.

04Value functions

The state-value function is the expected return starting from $s$ under policy $\pi$; the action-value function fixes the first action as well:

$$v_{\pi}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[G_t\mid S_t=s\right],\qquad q_{\pi}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[G_t\mid S_t=s,\,A_t=a\right].$$

They are related by a simple average and a simple lookahead:

$$v_{\pi}(s)=\sum_{a}\pi(a\mid s)\,q_{\pi}(s,a),\qquad q_{\pi}(s,a)=\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma\,v_{\pi}(s')\big].$$

The second identity is the load-bearing one: the value of an action is the reward it yields plus the discounted value of wherever it lands. That recursion is all of reinforcement learning.

Two functions, one recursion. $q_{\pi}$ is the more primitive object — $v_{\pi}$ is just $q_{\pi}$ averaged over the policy. The whole book alternates between them: value-based methods lean on $q$, actor–critic methods on both.

05The Bellman expectation equation

Split the return into the immediate reward plus the discounted rest, then use the Markov property to throw away the history:

$$ \begin{aligned} v_{\pi}(s) &= \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[G_t \mid S_t=s\right] \\[2pt] &= \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[R_{t+1}+\gamma G_{t+1} \mid S_t=s\right] \\[2pt] &= \sum_{a}\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[G_{t+1}\mid S_{t+1}=s'\right]\big] \\[2pt] &= \sum_{a}\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma\,v_{\pi}(s')\big] \end{aligned} $$

The third line is exactly where the Markov property is spent: conditioning on the entire history collapses to conditioning on $S_{t+1}=s'$.

Evaluating a policy is a linear solve

For a fixed policy the expectation is linear in $v_\pi$, so the Bellman equation is once again a linear system:

$$v_{\pi}=r^{\pi}+\gamma P^{\pi}v_{\pi}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\;v_{\pi}=(I-\gamma P^{\pi})^{-1}r^{\pi}\;}$$

Read that back: a policy is fully evaluated by inverting a single matrix — fixing $\pi$ turns the MDP back into an MRP, and MRPs are solved in closed form.

06The Bellman optimality equation and optimal policies

Define $v_{*}(s)=\max_{\pi}v_{\pi}(s)$ and $q_{*}(s,a)=\max_{\pi}q_{\pi}(s,a)$. They satisfy the Bellman optimality equations:

$$v_{*}(s)=\max_{a}\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma\,v_{*}(s')\big],\qquad q_{*}(s,a)=\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\Big[r+\gamma\max_{a'}q_{*}(s',a')\Big].$$

A policy is greedy with respect to $v_*$ if at every state it takes an action attaining the maximum, equivalently $\pi_*(s)\in\arg\max_a q_*(s,a)$. Greedy is not a heuristic here — it is exactly optimal:

$$\pi_{*}(s)\in\arg\max_{a}\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma\,v_{*}(s')\big].$$
A deterministic optimal policy always exists. Fix $v_*$ and, at every state, take an action that attains the max. The resulting stationary, deterministic $\pi_*$ satisfies $v_{\pi_*}=v_*$: substitute $v_*$ into the expectation and the greedy choice reproduces the max at every state, so $v_{\pi_*}$ obeys the same fixed-point equation that $v_*$ uniquely solves. Randomising over equally good actions can only dilute, never improve.
The $\max$ is the whole story. Under a fixed policy the operator is linear and invertible. Fold in $\max_a$ and it becomes piecewise-linear and nonlinear — no matrix inverse exists. We iterate instead, and iteration is safe because the right-hand side is a $\gamma$-contraction: $$\|\mathcal{T}v-\mathcal{T}u\|_{\infty}\le\gamma\|v-u\|_{\infty}.$$ Banach's fixed-point theorem then gives a unique $v_*$ and geometric convergence at rate $\gamma$ — that is value iteration, the engine behind the explorer below.

Cheat sheet

$$\begin{aligned} G_t &= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}R_{t+k+1}\\[2pt] q_{\pi}(s,a) &= \sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\Big[r+\gamma\sum_{a'}\pi(a'\mid s')\,q_{\pi}(s',a')\Big]\\[2pt] v_{\pi}(s) &= \sum_{a}\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma\,v_{\pi}(s')\big]\\[2pt] v_{*}(s) &= \max_{a}\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma\,v_{*}(s')\big]\\[2pt] \pi_{*}(s) &\in \arg\max_{a}\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma\,v_{*}(s')\big] \end{aligned}$$

Everything downstream — dynamic programming, Monte Carlo, TD learning, Q-learning, policy gradients, PPO — is an approximate way of computing, sampling, or differentiating one of these five lines.
