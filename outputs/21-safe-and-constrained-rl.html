Safe and Constrained RL

Every algorithm so far optimised one number. Real deployments charge for two — the reward you want and the damage you tolerate — and this chapter is about trading the second for the first.

01Constrained MDPs

An MDP scores a policy with one number. A deployment charges for two: how good it is, and how much damage it does getting there. A constrained MDP carries both.

$$\mathcal M=(\mathcal S,\mathcal A,P,r,c,\gamma),\qquad \max_{\pi}\;J(\pi)=\mathbb E_\pi\!\Big[\textstyle\sum_t\gamma^{t}r_t\Big]\quad \text{s.t.}\quad J_c(\pi)=\mathbb E_\pi\!\Big[\textstyle\sum_t\gamma^{t}c_t\Big]\le d .$$

Here $c_t\ge0$ is a cost — a collision, a torque overrun, a dollar — and $d$ is the budget. The feasible set $\Pi_d=\{\pi:J_c(\pi)\le d\}$ is a thin corner of policy space, and the reward-optimal policy often lives outside it.

Optimality is not safety. The greedy policy of the underlying MDP can be its worst violator, and patching it afterwards is the failure the constraint was meant to prevent.

Assume $\Pi_d\ne\emptyset$ and $c$ bounded. A CMDP is secretly a linear program over occupancy measures, so under Slater's condition the duality gap is zero — which makes the single multiplier below exact rather than a heuristic. The LP view has a second consequence: the optimal policy may have to randomise, and the best deterministic policy can be strictly worse — unlike an unconstrained MDP.

02The Lagrangian and the price of safety

Free the constraint by pricing it. Fold cost into one scalar with a multiplier $\lambda\ge0$:

$$\mathcal L(\pi,\lambda)=J(\pi)-\lambda\big(J_c(\pi)-d\big).$$

The constrained problem is its saddle point — max over policies, min over prices:

$$\max_{\pi}\min_{\lambda\ge0}\mathcal L(\pi,\lambda)\;=\;\min_{\lambda\ge0}\max_{\pi}\mathcal L(\pi,\lambda).$$

Read the inner problem back: for a fixed $\lambda$, maximising $\mathcal L$ is ordinary RL on the shifted reward $r_t-\lambda c_t$. The constraint has become a toll.

And $\lambda$ is a literal price — the price of safety. At the optimum the two gradients line up:

$$\nabla J(\pi^\star)=\lambda^\star\nabla J_c(\pi^\star),\qquad \lambda^\star\big(J_c(\pi^\star)-d\big)=0 .$$

So $\lambda^\star>0$ only when the budget binds, and then it is the marginal reward of loosening the constraint — the shadow price of safety. The dual function $g(\lambda)=\max_\pi\mathcal L(\pi,\lambda)$ is convex, and the correct price is its minimiser:

$$g(\lambda)=\max_{\pi}\mathcal L(\pi,\lambda),\qquad \lambda^\star=\arg\min_{\lambda\ge0}g(\lambda).$$
The dual is one-dimensional. However large $\pi$ is, choosing the price is a root-find.

03Primal–dual updates and CPO

Two variables, one loop. Alternate an exact primal maximisation with a gradient step on the price:

$$\pi_{k}=\arg\max_{\pi}\mathcal L(\pi,\lambda_k),\qquad \lambda_{k+1}=\big[\lambda_k+\eta\,\big(J_c(\pi_k)-d\big)\big]_+.$$

The second line is dual ascent — gradient descent on the convex $g$, whose slope is $g'(\lambda_k)=d-J_c(\pi_k)$. Overspend and the price rises; come in under budget it falls: a market clearing demand against a fixed supply. The projection $[\cdot]_+$ keeps the price non-negative.

Choose $\eta$ small enough and the pair reaches a saddle point; too large and the price rings across the boundary. Nearly every practical constrained method is this loop.

CPO: a trust region with a second constraint

CPO (constrained policy optimization) makes the primal step a trust region, so each update moves little and the budget holds — approximately — after every update, not just at the fixed point:

$$\max_\theta\;\mathbb E\!\Big[\tfrac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_{\theta_k}(a\mid s)}A^{\pi_{\theta_k}}(s,a)\Big] \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \bar D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_k}\Vert\pi_\theta)\le\delta,\; J_c(\pi_{\theta_k})+\tfrac{1}{1-\gamma}\mathbb E\!\Big[\tfrac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\theta_k}}A_c^{\pi_{\theta_k}}\Big]\le d .$$

TRPO with a second, linear constraint on cost; the dual now carries two multipliers — trust and safety — and CPO solves the resulting quadratic in closed form. PPO-Lagrangian is the cheap cousin: clip the ratio, subtract $\lambda c_t$ from the reward, animate $\lambda$ by the rule above.

04Shielding and model-based guarantees

A price is soft: it discourages violations but never forbids them. A shield is hard, sitting between policy and environment and giving unsafe actions zero mass:

$$\pi^{\text{shield}}(a\mid s)=\frac{\pi(a\mid s)\,\mathbf 1\{a\in\mathcal A_{\text{safe}}(s)\}}{\displaystyle\sum_{a'}\pi(a'\mid s)\,\mathbf 1\{a'\in\mathcal A_{\text{safe}}(s)\}} .$$

With a known model the mask $\mathcal A_{\text{safe}}(s)=\{a:T(s,a)\cap\mathcal S_{\text{bad}}=\emptyset\}$ forbids any action with a successor in the bad set; a probabilistic variant keeps only actions whose reach probability stays below a tolerance $\delta$. One step of lookahead is not enough in general: $\mathcal S_{\text{bad}}$ must include every state from which the bad set can no longer be avoided, which a shield computes offline as a safety game.

The guarantee is about the model, not the learner. If the mask is correct, no policy — however badly trained — can select a masked action, so the cost bound holds from step one. The price is conservatism: a too-tight mask can make the goal unreachable.

05Curriculum induction for safe exploration

Constraint satisfaction has to survive learning, not just deployment. Every exploratory action is a chance to violate, and random exploration is worst exactly where the state space is dangerous.

Curriculum induction grows the task instead of the policy: begin where success is easy and failure is cheap, and widen the reachable set as competence arrives.

$$\mathcal D_{k+1}=\begin{cases} \mathcal D_k\cup\{\text{harder tasks}\}, & \hat J_c(\pi_k)\le d,\\[2pt] \mathcal D_k, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

A curriculum turns an infeasible exploration problem into a sequence of feasible ones. The mechanism is generic: shrink the hazard set, anneal the start outward from the goal, or cap the horizon. In Turchetta et al.'s version a teacher also intervenes, resetting the learner before it violates, so training itself is safe. Below, two identical learners differ only in the order of exposure. The curriculum arm banks far fewer violations while it is still incompetent — 4 against 43 by episode 100 — and once both are competent they violate at the same rate ($\varepsilon$-exploration never stops), so the early gap stays.

06The safety budget

Put it together. A car runs a fixed-length trip; going faster covers more ground but risks a collision. Reward is progress, cost is the collision rate, and the budget $d$ is the slider — a CMDP solved by a live price.

$$r(v)=v,\qquad \mathbb E[c_t\mid v]=\kappa v^2,\qquad \max_{v}\;T\,v\;\;\text{s.t.}\;\;\kappa T\,v^2\le d \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; v^\star=\sqrt{\tfrac{d}{\kappa T}},\qquad \lambda^\star=\frac{1}{2\kappa v^\star}.$$

Expected collisions over the trip are $\kappa T v^2$, equal to $d$ at the optimum. Tighten the budget and the price rises, speed falls, and dual ascent — here a multiplicative step on $\log\lambda$, which copes with $\lambda^\star$ spanning roughly $10$ to $63$ — makes the policy conservative.

The multiplier travels further than safety: in the next chapter the same primal–dual machinery returns as RLHF, where the cost is the KL divergence from a reference policy and $\lambda$ becomes the coefficient $\beta$ that keeps the aligned model from drifting — adaptive-KL PPO even tunes it by a rule of this shape.
