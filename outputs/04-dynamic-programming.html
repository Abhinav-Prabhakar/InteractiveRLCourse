Dynamic Programming

When you know the model, you can compute the optimal policy exactly — by turning the Bellman equations into fixed-point iterations you can literally watch converge.

01Iterative policy evaluation

Chapter 3 gave us the Bellman expectation equation. For a fixed policy it is a linear system, solvable in closed form — but inverting a matrix once per policy is wasteful, and it breaks the moment the model is large. Dynamic programming replaces the solve with a repeated update.

$$v_{k+1}(s)=\sum_a \pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma\, v_k(s')\big]$$

This is the Bellman expectation backup. The right-hand side reads the current estimate $v_k$, never the true $v_\pi$ — that substitution is the entire trick. Stacking states into a vector makes a full sweep one matrix operation:

$$\mathbf{v}_{k+1}=\mathbf{r}_\pi+\gamma P_\pi\mathbf{v}_k$$

Read that last line back: one sweep replaces every state's value with a one-step lookahead, evaluated using the stale values still sitting on the right-hand side.

Why it must converge. The update is affine, with linear part $\gamma P_\pi$. Because every row of $P_\pi$ sums to one, $\|\gamma P_\pi\|_\infty=\gamma<1$, so the operator is a $\gamma$-contraction. Iterating from any starting $v_0$ converges geometrically to the unique fixed point — and the fixed point is exactly the $v_\pi$ that Chapter 3 defined.

02Policy improvement

Suppose we trust $v_\pi$. At each state, ask whether a different first action would do better, holding the policy fixed thereafter. That question is answered by the action-value function:

$$q_\pi(s,a)=\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma\, v_\pi(s')\big]$$

Act greedily on it and you get a new policy:

$$\pi'(s)=\arg\max_a\, q_\pi(s,a)$$

The policy improvement theorem says this can only help. If $q_\pi(s,\pi'(s))\ge v_\pi(s)$ at every state, then $v_{\pi'}(s)\ge v_\pi(s)$ everywhere, and strictly wherever the first bound is strict. The proof is an inequality you keep re-reading one step further down the trajectory:

$$ \begin{aligned} v_\pi(s) &\le q_\pi(s,\pi'(s))\\[2pt] &= \mathbb{E}\!\big[R_{t+1}+\gamma\, v_\pi(S_{t+1})\mid S_t=s,\,A_t=\pi'(s)\big]\\[2pt] &\le \mathbb{E}\!\big[R_{t+1}+\gamma\, q_\pi(S_{t+1},\pi'(S_{t+1}))\mid S_t=s,\,A_t=\pi'(s)\big]\\[2pt] &\le \mathbb{E}_{\pi'}\!\big[R_{t+1}+\gamma R_{t+2}+\gamma^2 v_\pi(S_{t+2})\mid S_t=s\big]\\[2pt] &\;\;\vdots\\[2pt] &\le \mathbb{E}_{\pi'}\!\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}R_{t+k+1}\;\middle|\;S_t=s\right]=v_{\pi'}(s) \end{aligned} $$

The third line is exactly where the bound is spent: the same inequality is reapplied to the next state, then the next, until every action on the path is greedy and the expectation is the return of $\pi'$ itself.

Improvement is monotone but not immediately optimal. A single greedy step never lowers any value, yet it can stop short of $\pi_*$ when the underlying $v_\pi$ was itself inaccurate. The cure is to iterate the two halves — which is the next section.

03Policy iteration

Chain the two operations. Evaluate a policy, improve it greedily, evaluate the new one, and repeat. Every improvement step yields a strictly better policy unless the old one was already optimal, so the sequence strictly increases at each change and cannot cycle; a finite MDP has only finitely many deterministic policies.

$$\pi_0 \xrightarrow{\;E\;} v_{\pi_0} \xrightarrow{\;I\;} \pi_1 \xrightarrow{\;E\;} v_{\pi_1} \xrightarrow{\;I\;} \cdots \xrightarrow{\;I\;} \pi_* \xrightarrow{\;E\;} v_*$$

Policy iteration therefore terminates in a finite number of outer loops, at a policy whose value satisfies the Bellman optimality equation — meaning at $\pi_*$ and $v_*$.

The evaluation is the expensive half. Each exact evaluation is a full MRP solve, and the loop may run it many times. In practice the policy stops changing after a handful of outer loops, so the wall-clock is dominated by the inner solves — not by the number of policies.

04Value iteration

Do we really need to evaluate all the way to convergence before improving? No. Truncate the evaluation at a single sweep and fold the improvement into the same update, replacing the policy average by a max:

$$v_{k+1}(s)=\max_a\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma\, v_k(s')\big]$$

Value iteration is policy iteration with the inner loop cut to length one. It is nothing more than the Bellman optimality equation used as an assignment rather than as a system to solve.

One sweep is enough. Waiting for $v_\pi$ to be accurate is unnecessary: the $\max$ pulls the estimate toward optimality on every single pass, and the operator that does the pulling is the same $\gamma$-contraction — so the iterates land on $v_*$.

Read the update once more: take a one-step lookahead under every action, keep the best, and use the old estimate of the successor. When the largest change $\|v_{k+1}-v_k\|_\infty$ drops below your threshold, stop and harvest the greedy policy.

05Asynchronous DP and GPI

Synchronous sweeps update every state from a snapshot of the previous sweep. Asynchronous DP updates states one at a time, in any order, using whatever values happen to be current. Both converge; asynchronous variants usually converge faster, because fresh information propagates the moment it is produced.

Two flavours earn their names. In-place DP overwrites each value as it walks the sweep, instead of writing into a copy. Prioritized sweeping maintains a queue ordered by how much each state's value would change, and backs up the most wrong state first.

Step back and the two halves reveal one machine. Evaluation drives $v$ toward $v_\pi$; improvement drives $\pi$ toward greedy with respect to $v$. Each disturbs the other, and they settle only when both are mutually consistent — at the fixed point of the Bellman optimality equation. This is generalized policy iteration (GPI):

Everything tabular is GPI. Policy iteration, value iteration, and the model-free methods of the next chapters are the same two arrows — they differ only in how far each arrow is driven before the other takes over, and in whether the lookahead uses the true model or a sample.

06Why it converges

Write the value-iteration update as an operator on value functions,

$$(\mathcal{T}v)(s)=\max_a\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma\, v(s')\big]$$

and let $u$ be any other value function. We claim $\mathcal{T}$ is a $\gamma$-contraction in the sup norm. Fix a state $s$ and, for each action $a$, abbreviate the two lookaheads as $f_a=\sum_{s',r}p\,[r+\gamma v(s')]$ and $g_a=\sum_{s',r}p\,[r+\gamma u(s')]$. The $\max$ is non-expansive, which is the one fact the whole argument rests on:

$$\big|\max_a f_a-\max_a g_a\big|\le\max_a|f_a-g_a|$$

Apply it, then push the absolute value inside and use the triangle inequality:

$$ \begin{aligned} \big|(\mathcal{T}v)(s)-(\mathcal{T}u)(s)\big| &\le \max_a\Big|\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\,\gamma\big[v(s')-u(s')\big]\Big|\\[2pt] &\le \gamma\max_a\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)\,\big|v(s')-u(s')\big|\\[2pt] &\le \gamma\max_{s'}\big|v(s')-u(s')\big|=\gamma\|v-u\|_\infty \end{aligned} $$

The second line is exactly where the triangle inequality is spent: the immediate reward and the $\gamma$ factor appear in both $f_a$ and $g_a$, so they cancel, and only the value difference survives inside the absolute value. Taking the max over $s$ gives the contraction:

$$\|\mathcal{T}v-\mathcal{T}u\|_\infty\le\gamma\,\|v-u\|_\infty$$
Contraction buys everything. By Banach's fixed-point theorem a $\gamma$-contraction on a complete space has a unique fixed point $v_*$, and iteration from any $v_0$ obeys $\|\mathcal{T}^{k}v_0-v_*\|_\infty\le\gamma^{k}\|v_0-v_*\|_\infty$ — geometric convergence at rate $\gamma$. Uniqueness does double duty: it forces $v_*$ to be the optimal value function and the greedy policy to be $\pi_*$.

Cheat sheet

$$\begin{aligned} v_{k+1}(s) &= \sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma v_k(s')\big]\\[2pt] q_\pi(s,a) &= \sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma v_\pi(s')\big]\\[2pt] \pi'(s) &= \arg\max_a q_\pi(s,a)\\[2pt] v_{k+1}(s) &= \max_a\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\big[r+\gamma v_k(s')\big]\\[2pt] \|\mathcal{T}v-\mathcal{T}u\|_\infty &\le \gamma\|v-u\|_\infty \end{aligned}$$

Everything that removes the model — Monte Carlo, TD learning, Q-learning, and the function-approximation methods beyond — is a way of estimating one of these five lines from samples instead of from $p(s',r\mid s,a)$.
