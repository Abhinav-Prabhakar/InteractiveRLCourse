01Behavioral cloning

The simplest way to learn from demonstrations is to treat them as labels. Behavioral cloning (BC) fits a policy to expert state–action pairs by ordinary supervised learning:

$$\min_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[-\log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\big]$$

No reward, no Bellman backup, no exploration — just classification on the expert's data $\mathcal{D}=\{(s_i,a_i)\}$. When the demonstrations are rich and the horizon is short this is remarkably strong, and it is the workhorse behind a lot of applied imitation today.

What cloning buys, and what it costs. The loss above assumes the pairs are i.i.d. and that the learner will be evaluated on the same distribution it was trained on. At deployment the second assumption is false: the learner's own actions decide which states it sees next. That single broken assumption is the entire difficulty of imitation.

02The compounding-error problem

Train on the expert's states, then act in your own. A state the expert never visits carries no training signal, so the policy there is arbitrary — and its mistakes push the agent into still more unseen states. Errors do not stay put; they change the distribution of future inputs.

Make that quantitative. Suppose the learner errs with probability $\varepsilon$ per step on average, and that any deviation from the expert's trajectory is costly. By step $t$ the learner may already have drifted, so the probability of being off-distribution at $t$ grows roughly linearly. Sum that over the horizon:

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\text{cost}] &= \sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\big(\text{off-distribution at step } t\big) \\[2pt] &\;\le\; \sum_{t=1}^{T}\varepsilon\, t \;=\; \varepsilon\,\frac{T(T+1)}{2} \;=\; O\!\left(\varepsilon T^{2}\right). \end{aligned} $$

The second line is exactly where the i.i.d. assumption is spent: a single-step error rate $\varepsilon$ becomes a probability of having already left the expert's states, and summing it over the horizon squares the dependence on $T$.

Read that last line back: the cost is $\varepsilon$ per step, but each step inherits all the drift accumulated before it, so the total grows with the horizon quadratically.

The feedback loop is the whole story. BC does not fail because the classifier is bad — it can be near-perfect in-distribution. It fails because the learner's mistakes are fed back as inputs, and the training set never contained the states those mistakes produce.

03DAgger: fixing the distribution

DAgger (Dataset Aggregation) repairs the distribution by construction. Roll out the current policy, ask the expert to label the states the learner actually visits, add those pairs to the dataset, and retrain. Repeat. The learner is supervised on its own mistakes, so each error is corrected where it happens instead of accumulating.

$$\min_\theta\;\sum_{i=1}^{N}\;\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_{\pi_i}}\big[-\log \pi_\theta\big(a^{*}(s)\mid s\big)\big]$$

Here $d_{\pi_i}$ is the state distribution induced by the policy at round $i$, and $a^{*}(s)$ is the expert's action queried at that state. Training on $\bigcup_i d_{\pi_i}$ instead of only the expert's own $d_{\pi^*}$ removes the compounding term:

$$\text{cost}_{\text{BC}}=O\!\left(\varepsilon T^{2}\right)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{cost}_{\text{DAgger}}=O\!\left(\varepsilon T\right).$$

The same per-step error $\varepsilon$, one fewer factor of $T$: trained on the distribution it will actually face, the learner's error stays bounded.

04Inverse reinforcement learning

BC copies behavior but never asks why. Inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) asks a deeper question: what reward is the expert optimizing? Recover it and you can re-solve the MDP — reusing the same objective under new dynamics, new start states, and planner-grade generalization the demonstrations never showed.

The problem is under-determined. Many rewards explain one optimal policy, so IRL is only ever identified up to a family:

$$R'(s,a)=R(s,a)+\gamma\,\Phi(s')-\Phi(s)\quad\text{for any potential }\Phi \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{same optimal policy}.$$
Reward is only identifiable up to transformation. Adding a constant leaves every optimal action unchanged, and any potential-based shaping $\gamma\Phi(s')-\Phi(s)$ does too (Ng, Harada & Russell). Every honest IRL method returns a reward class, not a unique function — which is why the panel below compares the recovered map to the truth after centering both.

The canonical method is Maximum Entropy IRL. Instead of demanding that the expert be exactly optimal, it assumes the expert is Boltzmann-rational: among trajectories, higher reward is exponentially more likely.

$$P(\tau\mid\theta)=\frac{\exp\big(R_\theta(\tau)\big)}{Z(\theta)},\qquad Z(\theta)=\sum_{\tau}\exp\big(R_\theta(\tau)\big).$$

Maximize the log-likelihood of the expert's trajectories and differentiate. The partition function is the only hard part, and its log-derivative is a familiar expectation:

$$ \begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta\log P(\tau\mid\theta) &= \nabla_\theta R_\theta(\tau)-\nabla_\theta\log Z(\theta)\\[2pt] &= \nabla_\theta R_\theta(\tau)-\mathbb{E}_{\tau'\sim P(\cdot\mid\theta)}\big[\nabla_\theta R_\theta(\tau')\big]. \end{aligned} $$

The second line is exactly where the partition function is spent: $\nabla_\theta\log Z=\mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta R_\theta]$ under the model's own distribution. Take a linear reward over one-hot state features, $R_\theta(\tau)=\sum_{s}\theta_s\,\phi(s)$, so $\nabla_\theta R_\theta(\tau)$ is just the state-visitation count $\phi(\tau)$. Averaging over the dataset gives the update rule IRL actually runs:

$$\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L} =\mathbb{E}_{\tau_E}\big[\phi(\tau)\big]-\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\big[\phi(\tau)\big] =\mu_{\text{expert}}-\mu_\theta .$$

Read it back: the gradient raises the reward of states the expert dwells in more than the learner does, and lowers it wherever the learner lingers. Push until the two visitation distributions match — feature matching — and the recovered reward is one the expert's behavior is optimal for.

05Reward recovery in a gridworld

The gradient above is a complete algorithm. Hold a reward table $\theta$, solve for the Boltzmann-optimal policy and its discounted visitation $\mu_\theta$, then nudge $\theta$ toward the expert counts: $\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha(\mu_{\text{expert}}-\mu_\theta)$. 06Adversarial imitation and IQ-Learn

Feature matching needs the model's visitation $\mu_\theta$, which for a deep policy means solving an inner RL problem. GAIL (Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning) sidesteps the reward model entirely: train a discriminator to tell expert state–action pairs from the learner's, then hand the discriminator's score to the policy as its reward.

$$\min_\pi\max_D\;\mathbb{E}_{\tau_E}\big[\log D(s,a)\big] +\mathbb{E}_{\tau_\pi}\big[\log\big(1-D(s,a)\big)\big]-\lambda\,H(\pi), \qquad r(s,a)=\log D(s,a).$$

The discriminator is never told the true reward; it only ever sees demonstrations. Imitating the expert is equivalent to matching its occupancy measure, and the adversarial game is a direct way to do that. The inner RL loop remains — that is the cost of drop-in adversarial training.

IQ-Learn removes the adversary. It keeps one function, a $Q$, and defines the reward implicitly from the soft Bellman equality, so training is a single stable objective instead of a min–max game: $$r(s,a)=Q(s,a)-\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'\sim P(\cdot\mid s,a)}\big[V(s')\big].$$ Maximizing $Q$ on expert transitions — regularized so the implied reward does not run away — recovers a reward-consistent $Q$ without any discriminator and without on-policy rollouts of the learned policy.

Cheat sheet

$$\begin{aligned} &\text{BC:}\quad \min_\theta\;\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[-\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\big]\\[3pt] &\text{compounding:}\quad \text{cost}_{\text{BC}}=O\!\left(\varepsilon T^{2}\right)\;\xrightarrow{\text{DAgger}}\;O\!\left(\varepsilon T\right)\\[3pt] &\text{MaxEnt gradient:}\quad \nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}=\mu_{\text{expert}}-\mu_\theta\\[3pt] &\text{GAIL:}\quad \min_\pi\max_D\;\mathbb{E}_{\tau_E}[\log D]+\mathbb{E}_{\tau_\pi}[\log(1-D)]-\lambda H(\pi)\\[3pt] &\text{IQ-Learn:}\quad r(s,a)=Q(s,a)-\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{s'}\big[V(s')\big] \end{aligned}$$

Everything downstream — constrained MDPs and safe RL, and the RLHF pipeline that turns human preferences into a reward — is a way of writing down, or learning, the reward that imitation only implies.
