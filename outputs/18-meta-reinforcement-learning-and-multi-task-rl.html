Meta-Reinforcement Learning
& Multi-Task RL

Learn over a distribution of tasks — so that a task it has never seen is solved in a handful of steps.

01The meta-RL problem

Every MDP so far was fixed. Meta-RL changes the unit of learning: not one task, but a task distribution.

$$\mathcal{T}\sim p(\mathcal{T}),\qquad \mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P_{\mathcal{T}},R_{\mathcal{T}},\gamma)$$

A meta-learner sees a stream of tasks and must produce an agent that, dropped into a fresh task, adapts quickly from a little experience. The quantity it optimizes is the meta-objective: expected return over the task distribution, *after* adaptation.

$$\theta^{*}=\arg\max_{\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}\sim p(\mathcal{T})} \Big[\ \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p_{\theta}(\tau\mid \mathcal{T})} \big[\textstyle\sum_{t=0}^{H} r_{\mathcal{T}}(s_t,a_t)\big]\ \Big]$$

Read it back: the outer expectation is over tasks, the inner one over trajectories the policy produces once it has been allowed to adapt to that task. Ordinary RL is the special case where $p(\mathcal{T})$ is a point mass.

Multi-task learning is not meta-learning. Multi-task trains one policy that is good on every training task. Meta-RL trains a *procedure* — a rule for turning experience into a good policy — so that the next task is cheap. Two independent choices remain: how the agent adapts (recurrence, gradients, a context variable), and how $\theta$ itself is optimized (policy gradient, evolution, meta-gradient).

02Recurrent meta-RL (RL²)

The cheapest place to put adaptation is inside the policy's memory. Feed the agent its own recent experience and let a recurrent network decide what matters.

$$u_t=(s_t,\ a_{t-1},\ r_{t-1}),\qquad h_t=f_{\theta}(h_{t-1},u_t),\qquad a_t\sim\pi_{\theta}(a_t\mid s_t,h_t)$$

The hidden state $h_t$ is a learned summary of everything seen so far in this task. Training is ordinary RL on the meta-objective: sample a task, roll out, push up the return — with the recurrent weights $W_h,W_u$ shared across every task.

$$\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}\Big[\textstyle\sum_{t}\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(a_t\mid s_t,h_t)\, \hat{A}_t\Big]$$

The hidden state is to the task distribution what the Markov state is to a single MDP: a sufficient statistic that makes the earlier history irrelevant. That is exactly why a recurrent policy can implement adaptation without taking a single gradient step at test time. The map $f_{\theta}$ is fixed; running it forward as data arrives *is* the adaptation. All the learning happened once, at meta-training time.

The recurrence is the adapter. Nothing about $f_{\theta}$ changes when a new task appears. The agent simply feeds observations in and reads a task-appropriate action out — the way $V(s)$ needs no re-solving once you are handed $s$.

03Gradient-based meta-RL (MAML)

If adaptation can be a few gradient steps, why not learn the *initialization* those steps start from? That is MAML: an inner loop that adapts, and an outer loop that scores the adapted result.

$$\begin{aligned} \text{inner:}\quad & \theta_i'=\theta-\alpha\,\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}(\theta)\\[2pt] \text{outer:}\quad & \theta\leftarrow\theta-\beta\,\nabla_{\theta}\sum_i \mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}\!\big(\theta-\alpha\,\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}(\theta)\big) \end{aligned}$$

$\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}$ is the task objective — negative return, or a policy-gradient surrogate. The inner step is fast adaptation; the outer step asks: from which $\theta$ do a few inner steps do the most good? Differentiating the outer loss reaches *through* the inner update by the chain rule:

$$\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}(\theta_i') =\Big(I-\alpha\,\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}(\theta)\Big) \,\nabla_{\theta'}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}(\theta')$$

The second term is exactly where MAML pays its cost: the Hessian of the inner objective. First-order variants — FOMAML, Reptile — drop it and optimize $\nabla_{\theta'}\mathcal{L}$ alone, buying cheapness at the price of an approximate meta-gradient.

The Hessian is the whole story. RL² adapts by running a network; MAML adapts by taking gradients. RL² learns one fixed function; MAML learns one fixed starting point — and the inner loop is a real optimization, so its derivative is what the meta-gradient must carry.

04Few-shot adaptation

A family of point-mass reaching tasks on a line: the agent starts at the centre and must drive to a goal it cannot see. The reward is dense — $r_t=-\lvert x_{t+1}-g\rvert$ — so the goal is knowable only from the *history* of rewards. A recurrent policy is meta-trained across five goals, then tested on one it never saw.

05Goal-conditioned policies & hindsight

Make the goal an input and one policy covers every task in the family. A goal-conditioned policy is just $\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s,g)$; the goal enters the reward.

$$\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s,g),\qquad r(s,a,g)=-\lVert s'-g\rVert$$

Sparse goal rewards make learning brutal: a random policy almost never reaches $g$, so almost every transition looks like failure. Hindsight experience replay (HER) fixes the labels, not the policy. A failed episode still *reached* the states along its path — so relabel those as goals.

$$g'\ \sim\ \{\,s_{t'}:t'\in\{t+1,\dots,T\}\,\},\qquad r'_t=r(s_t,a_t,g')$$

Every trajectory is a catalogue of goals that were achieved. Replaying it against those goals turns one failure into many successes.

Hindsight turns each failure into a lesson. The policy is unchanged; only the goal attached to each transition is swapped. Drag the transition slider — near the end of the trajectory the relabelled goal is a state the agent just reached, so the reward becomes an exact hit.

06Task inference & context variables

Tasks in a family usually differ along a few hidden coordinates. Collect them into a low-dimensional context $z$ that indexes the task, and let the agent infer $z$ from experience.

$$q_{\phi}(z\mid c_t),\qquad c_t=\{(s_i,a_i,r_i)\}_{i\le t}$$

The posterior $q_{\phi}$ is the probabilistic version of RL²'s hidden state: a belief over which task is being faced, sharpened online. Latent-context methods (PEARL and its descendants) train policy and inference together:

$$\max_{\theta,\phi}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}}\Big[ \mathbb{E}_{z\sim q_{\phi}(z\mid c^{\mathcal{T}})}\big[\hat{J}(\theta,z)\big] -\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(q_{\phi}(z\mid c^{\mathcal{T}})\,\|\,p(z)\big)\Big]$$

The return term rewards contexts that lead to good actions; the KL keeps the posterior honest and close to the prior. Below, observations arrive one at a time and the belief concentrates on the task that generated them.

Cheat sheet

$$\begin{aligned} \theta^{*} &= \arg\max_{\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{T}\sim p(\mathcal{T})} \big[\mathbb{E}_{\tau}\big[\textstyle\sum_t r_{\mathcal{T}}(s_t,a_t)\big]\big]\\[3pt] h_t &= f_{\theta}(h_{t-1},(s_t,a_{t-1},r_{t-1})),\qquad a_t\sim\pi_{\theta}(a_t\mid s_t,h_t)\\[3pt] \theta &\leftarrow \theta-\beta\,\nabla_{\theta}\textstyle\sum_i \mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}\!\big(\theta-\alpha\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{T}_i}(\theta)\big)\\[3pt] r'_t &= r(s_t,a_t,g'),\qquad g'\sim\{s_{t'}:t'>t\}\\[3pt] q_{\phi}(z\mid c_t) &\propto p(z)\prod_{i\le t} p(r_i\mid z),\qquad \pi_{\theta}(a\mid s,z) \end{aligned}$$

Everything downstream — goal-conditioned decision making, latent-context methods like PEARL, and the distributional and risk-sensitive objectives of the next chapter — is a way of conditioning one of these lines on more than the current state.
