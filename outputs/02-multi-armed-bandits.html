Multi-Armed Bandits

The explore–exploit dilemma stripped to one decision repeated — no state, no consequences, and every strategy you can run against another.

01The k-armed bandit problem

You face $k$ levers. Pull one, receive a reward drawn from a distribution fixed for that lever, repeat. There is no state and no consequence beyond the reward — just a decision you make again and again. This is the k-armed bandit problem, and it is where the central tension of reinforcement learning first appears.

Each action $a$ has an unknown expected reward

$$q_*(a)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[R_t\mid A_t=a\right].$$

If you knew $q_*(a)$ for every arm you would pull $\arg\max_a q_*(a)$ forever and be done. You do not, so you can only estimate values from sampled rewards — and that forces a choice at every step: exploit the arm that looks best, or explore to find out whether a better one exists. The estimate after $t$ steps is $Q_t(a)\approx q_*(a)$, and the greedy action is

$$A_t=\arg\max_a Q_t(a).$$
Exploration is not a detail you can skip. A greedy rule acting on noisy estimates is fragile: one lucky early pull can lock it onto a bad arm for the rest of the run. Almost everything clever in this chapter is a way to convert a little certain reward into information that is worth more later.

02Sample averages and the incremental update

The simplest estimate of an arm's value is the mean of the rewards you have actually seen from it. Let $N_t(a)$ be the number of times $a$ was pulled before step $t$. The sample-average estimate is

$$Q_t(a)=\frac{1}{N_t(a)}\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}R_i\,\mathbf{1}\!\left(A_i=a\right).$$

By the law of large numbers $Q_t(a)\to q_*(a)$ as $N_t(a)\to\infty$, so the estimate is eventually exact. But recomputing the whole sum on every pull is wasteful. There is a one-line recurrence. Write $Q_{n+1}$ for the average of the first $n$ rewards:

$$ \begin{aligned} Q_{n+1} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_i \\[2pt] &= \frac{1}{n}\Big(R_n+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}R_i\Big) \\[2pt] &= \frac{1}{n}\Big(R_n+(n-1)Q_n\Big) \\[2pt] &= Q_n+\frac{1}{n}\Big[R_n-Q_n\Big]. \end{aligned} $$

The third line is exactly where the induction is spent: the entire past $\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}R_i$ is compressed into the single remembered quantity $(n-1)Q_n$.

Read that last line back: every new observation nudges the estimate by the prediction error $R_n-Q_n$, scaled by a step size $1/n$ that shrinks as evidence accumulates. Replace $1/n$ with a constant $\alpha$ and you get the form that survives all the way to deep RL:

$$Q_{n+1}=Q_n+\alpha\Big[R_n-Q_n\Big].$$

Unrolled, a constant step size makes $Q$ an exponential recency-weighted average of the rewards,

$$Q_{n+1}=(1-\alpha)^{n}Q_1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha(1-\alpha)^{n-i}R_i,$$

which never fully forgets and never fully commits — exactly what you want when an arm's true value drifts over time.

03ε-greedy action selection

Estimates in hand, the cheapest cure for over-commitment is to be occasionally random. With probability $\varepsilon$ take a uniformly random arm; otherwise take the greedy one:

$$ A_t=\begin{cases} \arg\max_a Q_t(a) & \text{with probability } 1-\varepsilon\\[3pt] \text{a uniformly random arm} & \text{with probability } \varepsilon. \end{cases} $$

This is ε-greedy. Because every arm is selected with probability at least $\varepsilon/k$ it is pulled infinitely often, so every sample average converges; meanwhile the remaining $1-\varepsilon$ of pulls go to the current best guess. Set $\varepsilon=0$ and the method is pure exploitation; set $\varepsilon=1$ and it ignores everything it has learned.

The price of exploration is explicit and small. Even a purely greedy method pulls suboptimal arms some of the time — it just does so by accident. ε-greedy agrees to cap that cost at $\varepsilon$, and in practice $\varepsilon\approx 0.1$ is a robust default. Decaying $\varepsilon\to 0$ recovers optimal behaviour in the limit.

04Optimistic initial values

You can manufacture exploration for free by lying about where you start. Set every $Q_1(a)$ high — say $+5$ when the true values sit around $0$. The first greedy choice is arbitrary, but the moment you pull an arm its estimate falls toward reality, while the untouched arms keep their optimism and keep winning the $\arg\max$:

$$Q_1(a)=+5\ \text{for all }a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{greedy selection sweeps every arm before it settles.}$$

This is optimistic initialization, and it drives exploration through greed alone — no $\varepsilon$, no randomness. The whole mechanism is the mismatch between the initial estimate and reality: an arm is worth pulling precisely because its estimate has not yet been disappointed.

Optimism is a temporary subsidy on the unknown. It lasts only until each arm has been tried once, after which the estimates are honest and exploration stops. That makes it a poor fit for non-stationary problems, where arms you have already sampled must keep being re-checked.

05Upper-Confidence-Bound action selection

Random exploration wastes pulls on arms you already know are bad. A better idea is to explore where the estimate is uncertain. Put an upper confidence bound on each arm's value and be greedy with respect to the bound:

$$A_t=\arg\max_a\left[Q_t(a)+c\sqrt{\frac{\ln t}{N_t(a)}}\right].$$

The bracket is the upper-confidence-bound estimate: the sample mean plus a bonus that grows with how long you have been choosing and shrinks with how often $a$ has been sampled. An unpulled arm has an unbounded bonus and goes first; a much-pulled arm is trusted. The constant $c$ sets how many standard deviations of optimism you are willing to extend.

The square root is not a heuristic. It falls straight out of Hoeffding's inequality. For rewards in $[0,1]$, the sample mean $\bar X_n$ of $n$ i.i.d. draws obeys

$$\mathbb{P}\!\left[\bar X_n-\mathbb{E}[X]\ge u\right]\le e^{-2nu^2}.$$

Ask that the right-hand side be no larger than a failure probability $\delta$, and solve for $u$:

$$u=\sqrt{\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{2n}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q_*(a)\le Q_t(a)+\sqrt{\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{2N_t(a)}}\quad\text{with probability }1-\delta.$$

Let the failure probability shrink with time, $\delta=1/t^{4}$, so each arm is optimistic with probability at least $1-t^{-4}$; a union bound over the $k$ arms then keeps the whole testbed honest. Since $\ln(1/\delta)=4\ln t$,

$$q_*(a)\le Q_t(a)+\sqrt{\frac{2\ln t}{N_t(a)}},$$

which is exactly the UCB bonus with $c=\sqrt{2}$. The whole derivation is spent in the step from Hoeffding to $u$: the $\sqrt{\ln t/N}$ shape is a statistical confidence width, not a tuned formula.

Uncertainty, not chance, is the whole story. ε-greedy explores arms at random and pays for it. UCB explores the arms it is least sure about, so its regret grows like $\sqrt{kt\ln t}$ rather than linearly — while still acting greedily on the only quantity that deserves it, the bound.

06Gradient bandits and softmax policies

Every method so far chooses actions from value estimates. The alternative is to learn a policy directly, through a numerical preference $H_t(a)$ per arm and a softmax over those preferences:

$$\pi_t(a)=\frac{e^{H_t(a)}}{\sum_{b=1}^{k}e^{H_t(b)}}.$$

Preferences are never observed — only the actions they imply. After sampling $A_t\sim\pi_t$ and receiving $R_t$, take a stochastic-gradient step on the expected reward:

$$H_{t+1}(a)=H_t(a)+\alpha\Big(R_t-\bar{R}_t\Big)\Big(\mathbf{1}\!\left(a=A_t\right)-\pi_t(a)\Big),$$

where $\bar{R}_t$ is the average of the rewards seen so far, with $\bar{R}_1=R_1$. The factor $\mathbf{1}(a=A_t)-\pi_t(a)$ is what makes this a policy gradient: it raises the preference of the arm actually taken and lowers every other arm in proportion to how likely it already was.

The baseline $\bar{R}_t$ is safe because the softmax derivatives sum to zero. For any two arms $a$ and $b$,

$$\frac{\partial \pi_t(b)}{\partial H_t(a)}=\pi_t(b)\Big(\mathbf{1}\!\left(a=b\right)-\pi_t(a)\Big),$$

and since $\sum_b \pi_t(b)=1$, every constant baseline cancels: $\sum_b \pi_t(b)\,\partial_{H_t(a)}\pi_t(b)=0$. Subtracting $\bar{R}_t$ therefore changes the variance of the update, not its direction — the same baseline trick that returns as the critic's value function in Chapter 11.

07Contextual bandits

Real decisions usually arrive with a context: which ad to show depends on the user, which treatment to give depends on the patient. A contextual bandit hands the agent a feature vector $x_t$ before it acts, and the value becomes a function of the pair:

$$q_*(x,a)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[R_t\mid X_t=x,\,A_t=a\right],\qquad A_t=\arg\max_a Q_t(x_t,a).$$

Nothing about the exploration machinery changes: ε-greedy, UCB, and Thompson sampling all carry over once $Q_t(a)$ becomes $Q_t(x_t,a)$. What changes is that the estimate is now a function you must generalize across contexts, so function approximation enters (Chapter 9) and the problem stops being a table.

This is the bridge. A contextual bandit is a Markov decision process with exactly one step: observe, act once, collect a reward, done. Remove the context and you are back to plain bandits; let the context persist and respond to your action and you are in Chapter 3. The horizon — not the state — is what makes full reinforcement learning hard.

08Bandit Arena

One testbed, three strategies, measured the same way. Each arm's true value is drawn from $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and its rewards from $\mathcal{N}(q_*(a),1)$. Run all three side by side and ask which one accumulates the most reward — and the least regret:

$$\text{Regret}_T=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Big(\max_a q_*(a)-q_*(A_t)\Big).$$

Regret is the value left on the table by not pulling the best arm every step. A method that explores well produces a regret curve that bends down; one that explores badly keeps paying for the same mistake.

Cheat sheet

$$\begin{aligned} Q_{n+1} &= Q_n+\tfrac{1}{n}\big[R_n-Q_n\big] \quad\text{(or } Q_n+\alpha[R_n-Q_n]\text{)}\\[2pt] A_t &= \begin{cases}\arg\max_a Q_t(a) & 1-\varepsilon\\ \text{random} & \varepsilon\end{cases}\\[2pt] A_t &= \arg\max_a\Big[Q_t(a)+c\sqrt{\ln t\,/\,N_t(a)}\Big]\\[2pt] \pi_t(a) &= \frac{e^{H_t(a)}}{\sum_b e^{H_t(b)}},\qquad H_{t+1}(a)=H_t(a)+\alpha\big(R_t-\bar{R}_t\big)\big(\mathbf{1}(a=A_t)-\pi_t(a)\big) \end{aligned}$$

Everything downstream — Q-learning, DQN, policy gradients, PPO — is this same explore–exploit bargain, now played against a state that keeps moving underneath you.
