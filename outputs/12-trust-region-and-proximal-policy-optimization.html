Proximal Policy Optimization

A gradient tells you which way to step, never how far — TRPO and PPO are two ways of drawing that line.

01The step-size problem

Chapter 11 left us with an unbiased estimate of the policy gradient:

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!\left[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)\,\hat{A}(S_t,A_t)\right].$$

The vanilla update follows it with one learning rate, $\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$ — and that is exactly where things break. The gradient is evaluated under $\pi_\theta$, but the moment you apply it, the data in hand was collected by the old policy, not the new one.

The gradient is a direction, not a distance. It promises that $J$ increases for a small move; it says nothing about how far the increase survives. Step too far and the update is destructive — a probability collapses toward zero, its gradient vanishes with it, and the agent cannot walk itself back. Step too timidly and every rollout is wasted. What we need is a limit on how far the policy may move per update.

The next two algorithms fix the same defect. They keep the cheap first-order ascent direction and bolt on a bound on the size of the step — once explicitly, once implicitly.

02The surrogate objective

To reuse a batch collected under $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, correct for the off-policy mismatch with importance sampling. Pack the correction into a single number, the probability ratio:

$$r_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(A_t\mid S_t)},\qquad r_t(\theta_{\text{old}})=1 .$$

Reweighting the advantage by that ratio gives the surrogate objective:

$$L^{\text{CPI}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[r_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t\right].$$

Read it back: the surrogate credits an action in proportion to how much the update raises its probability, weighted by whether that action beat the average.

The surrogate is exact only at home. At $\theta=\theta_{\text{old}}$ we have $r_t=1$, and the surrogate matches the improvement $J(\theta)-J(\theta_{\text{old}})$ in both value (zero) and gradient — so a small step is safe. Away from home it stops tracking the objective: it still averages over the old policy's states. It is a first-order stand-in, trustworthy only while $r_t$ stays near $1$.

The surrogate is a straight line in $r_t$. For $\hat A_t>0$ it rises forever; for $\hat A_t<0$ it falls forever. Maximizing it over an unconstrained policy has no interior solution.

There is no brake. Follow this objective and the optimizer drives $r_t\to\infty$ whenever the sampled action was good, and $r_t\to 0$ whenever it was bad — long past the point where the ratio means anything. The missing ingredient is a leash.

03TRPO: a hard trust region

Trust Region Policy Optimization makes the leash explicit. Keep maximizing the surrogate, but forbid the action distribution from moving more than $\delta$ in KL divergence:

$$\max_\theta\;\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(A_t\mid S_t)}\,\hat{A}_t\right]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid S_t)\,\big\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid S_t)\right)\right]\le\delta .$$

The KL divergence measures how far the whole action distribution slid, averaged over the states in the batch. It is not a distance on parameters — two very different $\theta$ can induce the same policy, and a tiny parameter change can flip a near-deterministic action — which is exactly why the constraint lives on the induced distributions instead.

The theory behind the constraint: the surrogate minus a penalty $C\max_s D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ is a lower bound on the true improvement, so maximizing that bound can never decrease $J$. TRPO swaps the penalty for the average-KL constraint above — the spirit of the guarantee, not its letter. The optimizer first picks the steepest ascent direction under the Fisher information metric — the natural gradient $F^{-1}g$ — then scales it until it just touches the boundary of the trust region:

$$F=\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)^{\!\top}\right],\qquad \theta\leftarrow\theta+\sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{g^{\top}F^{-1}g}}\,F^{-1}g .$$

In practice $F^{-1}g$ is never formed: it is applied by conjugate gradient using Fisher-vector products, then a backtracking line search accepts the first candidate whose measured KL sits under $\delta$ and whose surrogate actually improves.

The constraint is the whole idea. Bound the KL and the surrogate becomes a promise rather than a guess — but the price is a second-order solver, dozens of matrix-vector products per update, and a line search that must be tuned. That cost is precisely what PPO removes.

04PPO: a soft trust region

Proximal Policy Optimization keeps TRPO's behavior and throws away its solver. Instead of an explicit KL constraint, it clips the ratio per sample and never lets the update profit from leaving the neighborhood:

$$L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[\min\!\left(r_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t,\;\operatorname{clip}\!\left(r_t(\theta),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon\right)\hat{A}_t\right)\right].$$

Read the two branches by the sign of the advantage. When $\hat A_t>0$ the action was better than average, so the objective is $\hat A_t\min(r_t,1+\epsilon)$: raising $r_t$ helps only up to $1+\epsilon$, then the curve goes flat. When $\hat A_t<0$ the action was worse, so the objective is $\hat A_t\max(r_t,1-\epsilon)$: lowering $r_t$ helps only down to $1-\epsilon$, then it too goes flat.

The $\min$ is the pessimism. It always takes the smaller of the unclipped and clipped objectives, so the update never earns credit for extrapolating past the trust region — and, symmetrically, it keeps every penalty for moving too far in a harmful direction, where the unclipped term is the smaller one.

Differentiate it. Since $\nabla_\theta r_t=r_t\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)$, the chain rule hands back Chapter 11's gradient with an indicator that switches it off exactly where the ratio has left the region:

$$\nabla_\theta L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\Big[\,\mathbf{1}\big\{(\hat A_t>0\wedge r_t<1+\epsilon)\;\vee\;(\hat A_t<0\wedge r_t>1-\epsilon)\big\}\;r_t\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)\,\hat{A}_t\Big].$$

The indicator is exactly where the trust region is spent: when it is zero — the ratio has traveled past the boundary in the direction that would help — the gradient for that sample vanishes, and the update simply walks away from it.

Why clipping is a trust region

Write $r_t-1=\big(\pi_\theta-\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\big)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$; it is the first-order change in the policy. The KL divergence is quadratic in that deviation to leading order,

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\,\big\|\,\pi_\theta\right)=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\left[(r_t-1)^2\right]+\mathcal{O}\!\left((r_t-1)^3\right).$$

So keeping $|r_t-1|\le\epsilon$ per sample bounds that second-order KL by roughly $\tfrac12\epsilon^2$: clipping replaces the KL ball with a box in ratio space, a first-order surrogate for the trust region TRPO enforces. It is a soft box — the clip removes the incentive to leave it, not the ability, so ratios can still drift out through shared parameters. Nothing is solved for; $\epsilon$ is the only trust-region knob, and the whole update is plain SGD on a loss.

The loop: run the policy for a batch of $N\times T$ steps, compute GAE advantages once, then take $K$ epochs of minibatch SGD on $L^{\text{CLIP}}$ against that frozen $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ — and repeat.

05Practical tricks

The clipped loss is only half the algorithm. The rest lives in details that decide whether it trains at all.

Advantage normalization

Normalize the advantages across the batch before they touch the surrogate:

$$\hat{A}_t\;\leftarrow\;\frac{\hat{A}_t-\mu_A}{\sigma_A+\varepsilon_{\text{num}}}.$$

This is not cosmetic. $\epsilon$ bounds the ratio, not the step: the gradient scales with $|\hat A_t|$, so at a fixed learning rate a task whose rewards are $100\times$ larger takes $100\times$ larger steps and blows through the clip band in one minibatch. Standardizing the batch fixes that scale once and for all.

Entropy bonus

Add a bounty for staying stochastic, and pair the actor with a value head for the critic:

$$L(\theta)=L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)-c_1\,L^{\text{VF}}(\theta)+c_2\,\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[H\!\left(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid S_t)\right)\right],\qquad L^{\text{VF}}=\mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\big(V_\theta(S_t)-V_t^{\text{targ}}\big)^2\right].$$

The whole thing is maximized: the value error is subtracted, the entropy added. The entropy term $H$ keeps the policy from collapsing to a near-deterministic action before it has explored, and $L^{\text{VF}}$ fits the value function that GAE leans on.

Learning-rate annealing

Linearly decay the step size toward zero over training. Early updates want to move; late updates want to settle, and annealing buys both without touching the clip range.

Scale is not a detail. Because $\epsilon$ is defined on the ratio, the surrogate's magnitude is load-bearing: normalize the advantage, anneal the rate, and cap the number of epochs per batch. Run too many epochs and the ratios keep drifting even on clipped samples, pulled along by the samples that still have gradient; the clip zeroes their gradient but cannot hold them in the box, and the trust region is gone.

Cheat sheet

$$\begin{aligned} r_t(\theta) &= \frac{\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(A_t\mid S_t)}\\[2pt] L^{\text{CPI}}(\theta) &= \mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[r_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t\right]\\[2pt] \text{TRPO:}\quad &\max_\theta\;\mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[r_t\,\hat{A}_t\right]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\,\big\|\,\pi_\theta\right)\right]\le\delta\\[2pt] L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) &= \mathbb{E}_{t}\!\left[\min\!\left(r_t\,\hat{A}_t,\;\operatorname{clip}(r_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\,\hat{A}_t\right)\right] \end{aligned}$$

Everything downstream — PPO-based RLHF, GRPO, the KL-regularized objective behind DPO — is a variation on this one move: take the policy-gradient step, then decide how far the policy is allowed to travel.
