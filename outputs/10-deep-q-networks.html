Deep Q-Networks
& Value-Based Deep RL

One network, one replay buffer, and one frozen copy of itself — enough to learn control without ever writing down the transition table.

01From a table to a network

Tabular Q-learning stores one number per state–action pair. That is fine for a $5\times5$ gridworld and hopeless past it: the table has $|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|$ entries and you will never visit them all.

$$\hat q_\theta(s,a)\approx Q_*(s,a),\qquad \theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad d\ll|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|$$

A function approximator replaces the table with parameters $\theta$. The deep Q-network (DQN) makes that approximator a neural network, so a single gradient step in one state nudges the estimate in many nearby states. That generalization is the gift.

It is also the curse. The tabular backup $Q(s,a)\leftarrow r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')$ assumed each entry could move without touching another. Share parameters, and three ingredients together — function approximation, bootstrapping, and off-policy data — can diverge. This is the deadly triad. DQN's reputation rests on the two cheap tricks that keep it in check, and this chapter is about them.

02The DQN objective

Keep the Bellman optimality target, but regress the network onto it. Each transition $(s,a,r,s')$ contributes a squared temporal-difference (TD) error, and the loss is its expectation over a replay buffer $\mathcal{D}$:

$$L(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{(s,a,r,s')\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\big(r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q_{\theta^-}(s',a')-Q_\theta(s,a)\big)^2\Big]$$

Two design decisions hide in that one line. The expectation runs over transitions drawn from $\mathcal{D}$ — past experience, not the current trajectory — and the target is computed with $Q_{\theta^-}$, a separate parameter vector rather than the $\theta$ being differentiated.

The gradient flows only through $Q_\theta(s,a)$; the target is held constant:

$$\nabla_\theta L(\theta)=-2\,\mathbb{E}_{(s,a,r,s')\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\underbrace{\big(r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q_{\theta^-}(s',a')\big)}_{\text{regression target }y}-Q_\theta(s,a)\Big]\nabla_\theta Q_\theta(s,a)$$

This is why it is the semi-gradient method: the $\nabla_\theta$ stops at the prediction. The target is a number the network is asked to hit, not a term it can differentiate through.

Freeze the target or chase your own tail. If $\theta^-=\theta$, every gradient step moves the target along with the prediction. The regression never settles — you are fitting a function to a moving version of itself. Replay and the target network are precisely the two mechanisms that stop the motion.

03Experience replay

An experience replay buffer stores transitions $(s,a,r,s')$ as they arrive and hands back random minibatches. Two things are bought at once.

The first is sample efficiency: a single transition can be reused across hundreds of gradient steps. The second is subtler — decorrelation. Consecutive transitions in a trajectory are nearly identical, and SGD on a correlated stream chases a moving objective and forgets what it learned a moment ago. A minibatch drawn at random across many past episodes looks far closer to i.i.d.:

$$i\sim\text{Uniform}\{1,\dots,|\mathcal{D}|\},\qquad \text{batch}=\{(s_i,a_i,r_i,s'_i)\}_{i\in\text{batch}}$$

Uniformity is the baseline. Prioritized experience replay, later in this chapter, replaces the uniform draw with one proportional to the TD error — but first see how much breadth the plain draw already buys.

04Target networks

The second stabilizer is the frozen copy $\theta^-$. Every $C$ gradient steps it is refreshed from $\theta$, and in between it does not move:

$$\theta^-\leftarrow\theta\ \text{ every }C\ \text{steps}\quad\text{(hard update)},\qquad \theta^-\leftarrow\tau\,\theta+(1-\tau)\,\theta^-\quad\text{(soft / Polyak)}$$

With $\theta^-$ frozen, the loss in Section 02 becomes an ordinary supervised regression onto fixed labels $y$. The labels are stale — that is precisely the point. They move slowly rather than every step, so the network can catch up before the target runs off.

Read the loss back with this in mind: $L$ is not the squared Bellman residual at the current parameters; it is the regression error against a slowly drifting snapshot of them.

The target network trades bias for stability. A copy that lags the true target is momentarily wrong. But a wrong target held still is something gradient descent can actually hit, whereas a correct target that moves every step is not. In the dashboard below you can watch the lag directly: the stored values freeze for $C$ steps, then jump.

Notice the division of labour. Replay attacks the correlation of the data; the target network attacks the correlation of the objective with its own parameters. Both are approximations, and both are load-bearing.

06Double, Dueling, Prioritized

Three repairs, each a few lines of code, each a large change in behaviour.

Double DQN

The target's $\max$ is an estimator, and the maximum of noisy estimates is biased upward. Even when every $\hat Q(s,a)$ is unbiased, the largest one is not:

$$\mathbb{E}\Big[\max_a\hat Q(s,a)\Big]\ \ge\ \max_a\mathbb{E}\big[\hat Q(s,a)\big]$$

Jensen's inequality, with $\max$ the convex function. A greedy target that chases this inflated estimate inflates it further, step after step. Double DQN splits the two roles: the online network picks the greedy action, the target network scores it.

$$y^{\text{Double}}=r+\gamma\,Q_{\theta^-}\!\Big(s',\ \arg\max_{a'}Q_\theta(s',a')\Big)$$

The selection and the evaluation are exactly where the two networks are spent: one chooses the action, the other reports its value — and they are no longer the same network grading its own homework.

The greedy $\max$ in the target is the whole story of the bias. Double DQN does not remove the $\max$; it only stops a single, correlated network from both proposing and pricing the action. Overestimation shrinks, and the policy stops locking onto actions that merely look best.

Dueling DQN

Some states are valuable no matter what you do; in others the choice of action dominates. Dueling DQN splits the value into a state value $V(s)$ and an advantage $A(s,a)=Q(s,a)-V(s)$:

$$Q_\theta(s,a)=\underbrace{V_\eta(s)}_{\text{state value}}+\underbrace{A_\psi(s,a)-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{a'}A_\psi(s,a')}_{\text{centred advantage}}$$

The mean subtraction is not cosmetic. The decomposition $Q=V+A$ is unidentifiable: add a constant to $V$, subtract it from every $A$, and $Q$ is unchanged. Pinning $\sum_a A(s,a)=0$ fixes the split — it makes $V$ the value of the average action.

Prioritized replay

Uniform replay spends equal effort on transitions the network already predicts well. Prioritized experience replay samples in proportion to the last TD error, and corrects the resulting bias with an importance weight:

$$P(i)=\frac{p_i^{\alpha}}{\sum_k p_k^{\alpha}},\qquad p_i=|\delta_i|+\varepsilon,\qquad w_i=\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|\,P(i)}\Big)^{\beta}$$

Here $\alpha$ sets how greedily the buffer favours surprising transitions, and $\beta$, annealed to $1$, removes the bias the non-uniform draw introduces. The three ideas compose: Rainbow stacks Double, Dueling, prioritized replay, $n$-step returns, and the distributional head treated next.

07Distributional DQN

Everything so far learns a number — the mean return. But the return is a random variable. Distributional RL learns its whole law:

$$Z(s,a)\ \sim\ \text{law of }G_t\mid S_t=s,\,A_t=a,\qquad Q(s,a)=\mathbb{E}\big[Z(s,a)\big]$$

The Bellman operator lifts from values to distributions. Expectation is linear, so it passes inside; the reward shift and the discount act on the random variable itself. Writing $\overset{D}{=}$ for equality in distribution,

$$(\mathcal{T}Z)(s,a)\ \overset{D}{=}\ r+\gamma\,Z(s',a'),\qquad s'\sim P(\cdot\mid s,a)$$

Read that back: the operator never touches means. It shifts and scales the entire distribution, and the value $Q$ is just one functional of its fixed point.

Representing a distribution

C51 fixes $N$ atoms on a grid $z_i=V_{\min}+i\,\Delta z$ and learns a categorical distribution over them; each Bellman backup is followed by a projection back onto the grid. QR-DQN instead learns $N$ quantiles $\theta_i(s,a)\approx F^{-1}_{Z(s,a)}(\tau_i)$ with the pinball loss

$$\rho_{\tau}(\delta)=\delta\big(\tau-\mathbb{1}[\delta<0]\big),\qquad \delta_{ij}=y_j-\theta_i(s,a)$$

and IQN makes the level $\tau\sim U(0,1)$ a continuous input, learning the full quantile function $Z_\tau(s,a)$ rather than a fixed set of atoms.

Why the tail, not the mean

A mean is one summary; risk lives in the tail. Once $Z$ is learned, any risk measure is a single functional of it. The canonical example is the conditional value at risk — the average of the worst $\tau$-fraction of outcomes:

$$\mathrm{CVaR}_{\tau}(Z)=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_0^{\tau}F^{-1}_{Z}(u)\,du$$

A risk-averse agent maximizes $\mathrm{CVaR}_\tau$; a risk-seeking one maximizes an upper-tail functional. Same learned distribution, different question asked of it — and different optimal actions.

A distribution is strictly more information than a mean. Two actions can share an expected return while one is a coin flip and the other is certain. Only $Z$ tells them apart, and only $Z$ lets the agent act on the difference.

Cheat sheet

$$\begin{aligned} L(\theta) &= \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\Big[\big(r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q_{\theta^-}(s',a')-Q_\theta(s,a)\big)^2\Big]\\[2pt] y^{\text{Double}} &= r+\gamma\,Q_{\theta^-}\big(s',\arg\max_{a'}Q_\theta(s',a')\big)\\[2pt] Q_\theta(s,a) &= V_\eta(s)+A_\psi(s,a)-\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|}\sum_{a'}A_\psi(s,a')\\[2pt] (\mathcal{T}Z)(s,a) &\overset{D}{=} r+\gamma Z(s',a')\\[2pt] \mathrm{CVaR}_{\tau}(Z) &= \tfrac{1}{\tau}\int_0^{\tau}F^{-1}_{Z}(u)\,du \end{aligned}$$

Everything downstream — REINFORCE, actor–critic, GAE, PPO — keeps this chapter's Bellman target but replaces the greedy $\max$ with a differentiable policy, optimizing $\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ directly: the value network becomes a critic, and the $\max$ becomes a gradient.
