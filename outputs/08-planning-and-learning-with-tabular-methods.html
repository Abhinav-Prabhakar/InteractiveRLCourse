Planning and Learning
with Tabular Methods

A model turns experience into a cheap, replayable simulator — and planning is just learning from that simulator. Dyna runs acting, learning and planning in one loop, squeezing far more out of every real step.

01Models and planning

A model is the agent's guess at the environment's dynamics — a map from $(s,a)$ to what comes next:

$$\hat P(s'\mid s,a)\approx\mathbb P\!\left(S_{t+1}=s'\mid S_t=s,A_t=a\right),\qquad \hat R(s,a)\approx\mathbb E\!\left[R_{t+1}\mid S_t=s,A_t=a\right].$$

A distribution model stores all of $\hat P$. A sample model stores only one observed successor $(s',r)$ per $(s,a)$ and replays it. The sample model is cheaper and pairs naturally with the value-based updates we already have.

Planning means improving a policy with experience generated by the model instead of the environment. Feed a sample of $\hat M$ into a Q-learning update and you have planned — without taking a real step.

The model is the agent's imagination, and planning is learning in it. Anything that learns from real experience can learn from imagined experience, because the update rule does not care where the transition came from.

02The Dyna architecture

Dyna-Q does all three jobs on every real step: act in the environment, learn from the real transition, and plan from $n$ simulated transitions. The update is the same Q-learning rule throughout:

$$Q(S,A)\;\leftarrow\;Q(S,A)+\alpha\Big[\,R+\gamma\max_{a}Q(S',a)-Q(S,A)\,\Big].$$

One line, applied $n+1$ times per step: once to the real $(S,A,R,S')$ the agent just saw, and $n$ times to pairs drawn from its model.

$$\underbrace{\text{act}}_{\text{real}}\;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{\text{learn}}_{\text{real}}\;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{\text{plan}\times n}_{\text{simulated}}$$

The real update and the simulated update are the same line of code. Planning is not a new algorithm; it is the same algorithm pointed at the model. Turn $n$ up and Dyna converts cheap computation into scarce real experience; turn it to zero and Dyna decays into plain Q-learning.

03Prioritized sweeping

Uniform Dyna spends planning on $(s,a)$ pairs whose values are already right. Spend it where the error is large — and the size of the error is something you already compute: the TD error.

$$p(s,a)=\Big|\;R+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')-Q(s,a)\;\Big|.$$

The priority is exactly the absolute TD error. Keep a queue keyed by $p$. Repeatedly pop the largest, back that pair up, then recompute $p$ for every predecessor pair whose target depends on the state you just changed. Sweeping in priority order drags information backward from reward along the paths that matter.

04Trajectory sampling

Which pairs should planning visit? Two extremes: back up every pair — an exhaustive sweep — or back up one sampled pair. Dyna-Q samples. A sample backup is cheaper, and unlike a sweep it still works when the state space is far too big to enumerate.

Trajectory sampling pushes the idea further: start at a state and follow the model's greedy policy for several steps, backing up each pair on the way. Computation then concentrates on the states the policy actually reaches instead of spreading across the whole grid.

On an episodic task only a sliver of the state space is ever visited under any sensible policy. Trajectory sampling buys accuracy exactly there and ignores the rest — which is why the local picture sharpens so much faster than it would under uniform sweeps.

05Monte Carlo tree search

MCTS plans without a value table at all. From the current state it grows a search tree by simulating trajectories, spending rollouts where the tree currently looks most promising. Each iteration has four phases — selection, expansion, simulation, backup — and selection is steered by UCT:

$$a^{*}=\arg\max_{a}\left[\,Q(s,a)+c\sqrt{\frac{\ln N(s)}{N(s,a)}}\;\right].$$

Read it back: $Q(s,a)$ exploits the action that has paid off; the $\sqrt{\cdot}$ term buys a trial for actions visited too rarely. As $N(s)$ grows the bonus shrinks, so the tree stops exploring and converges onto the best line of play.

MCTS needs only a simulator, not a value function — which is why it powers game engines, and, wrapped around a learned model, why it is the planner inside MuZero (Chapter 13).

06How much planning is enough?

Planning trades computation for real experience. The obvious question — how many planning steps $n$ should each real step buy? — has no clean formula, so measure it: run Dyna-Q on the gridworld above and record how many real steps it takes to reach a near-optimal policy, for growing $n$.

Balanced against its cost, a handful of planning steps captures almost all of the benefit; beyond that the curve flattens and extra planning is nearly free of value.
